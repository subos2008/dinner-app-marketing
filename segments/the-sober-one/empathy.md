# DEEP MODE

## 1. Persona Snapshot

I'm Tom, I'm 33. I stopped drinking two years ago after a stretch where I couldn't tell you the last weekend I'd been fully present for. It wasn't one rock-bottom moment — it was a slow accumulation of mornings I couldn't account for and friendships that only existed inside pubs. I'm fine now. Better than fine, actually. I just haven't figured out how to have a social life that doesn't revolve around the thing I don't do anymore.

## 2. Empathy Map

### Think & Feel

- "I'm proud of where I am. I just wish I didn't have to explain it every time I sit down at a table."
- Constant low-level calculation before any social event: will there be pressure to drink? Will I have to justify ordering a lime and soda? Will someone make it a thing?
- Quietly resentful that alcohol is the default social lubricant and nobody questions it
- Worries that people think he's boring now — or worse, that he thinks he's better than them
- Misses the easy camaraderie of rounds at the pub, not the drinking itself but the ritual of it
- Afraid that his old friendships were really just drinking partnerships that couldn't survive sobriety
- Sometimes feels more isolated sober than he ever did hungover
- Thinks about the version of himself five years ago who couldn't imagine a Friday without a pint, and feels both compassion and distance from that person

### Say & Do

- **When someone offers a drink:** "I'm alright thanks, just on the water tonight." Says "tonight" even though it's every night — because "tonight" doesn't invite follow-up questions.
- **To close friends:** "Yeah I'm good, I just feel better without it." Undersells two years of hard work to avoid the conversation becoming about him.
- **At a work event:** Arrives, gets a tonic water immediately so he's holding something, stays 90 minutes, leaves before the third round when the energy shifts.
- **On dating apps:** Doesn't mention sobriety in his profile. Mentions it on the second date. Watches their face when he says it.
- **With his sponsor / therapist:** Actually honest. "I'm lonely but I don't want to put myself in situations where I'm white-knuckling it through other people's drinking."
- Checks the menu of every restaurant before agreeing to go — not for food, for whether the vibe is "drinking venue that serves food" or "restaurant"
- Has a running list in his Notes app of places that do good mocktails or have a decent non-alcoholic selection
- Turns down invitations more than he wants to, then feels guilty about the turning down, then feels angry that the invitations always centre on drinking

### See

- Every pub garden in summer. Every rooftop bar advert. Every "bottomless brunch" offer. The entire social infrastructure of his city, built around alcohol.
- Friends' Instagram stories: pints, wine, cocktails, "first one of the evening," "we deserve this." Alcohol as reward, as ritual, as personality.
- Mocktail menus that are an afterthought — two options at the bottom of the drinks list, or worse, a patronising "virgin" section
- Sober influencer content that's either too evangelical ("SOBRIETY IS MY SUPERPOWER") or too fragile ("day 47, still struggling") — neither feels like him
- Other people at social events visibly loosening up after drink two while he stays at the same level of self-consciousness
- The moment at a dinner table when the wine arrives and everyone's attention shifts to pouring and tasting and he's just sitting there with his water

### Hear

- "Oh go on, one won't hurt." (It will.)
- "You're not drinking? Are you on antibiotics or something?" (No. I just don't drink. Why does there have to be a medical excuse?)
- "Fair play to you, I could never do that." (Said with admiration that accidentally highlights how abnormal his choice is.)
- "We're going to the new cocktail bar, you should come!" (They mean well. They've also just picked a venue where his sobriety is the centrepiece of his evening.)
- "You're so much more fun when — " Someone started that sentence once. They didn't finish it. He still hears it.
- "It's not a big deal, just come, you don't have to drink." (It isn't a big deal to you. You'll be four drinks in. I'll be four hours into performing ease.)
- The clink of glasses at a toast he participates in with a water. The half-second pause when someone notices.
- Silence from friends who stopped calling after he stopped going to the pub

## 3. Day in the Life — The Triggering Day

It's a Friday in early June. Tom wakes up at 6:45, clear-headed, like every morning for the last two years. He runs — three miles along the canal, AirPods in, a podcast about urban design because he's been into that lately. He showers. Eats eggs on toast. The morning is good. The mornings are always good. Mornings are the part of sobriety nobody argues with.

Work is fine. He's a project manager at an agency, twelve people in the office, mostly late twenties and early thirties. Good people. Funny, sharp, the kind of colleagues who make the day go fast. Around 2pm, the Slack channel lights up: someone's leaving. Not a close friend, but well-liked. Drinks tonight at The Alchemist. "Everyone's going."

He reads the message three times. Not because it's complicated. Because the calculation has already started.

The Alchemist is a cocktail bar. Not a restaurant that happens to serve drinks. A cocktail bar. The kind of place where the entire point is the drinks, where the lighting is low and the music is loud and every surface is designed to hold a glass. He could go. He's been to places like this before. But he knows exactly how the evening will unfold: he'll order a mocktail (if they have one), someone will comment on it, he'll say "just not drinking tonight," and then either nobody will mention it again (best case) or it'll become a topic (worst case) or someone three drinks in will lean over and say "seriously though, why?"

He types "Sounds good, I'll try to swing by!" which is the noncommittal yes he's perfected. It buys him four hours to decide.

At 5:30 the office empties fast. People are excited. It's sunny, it's Friday, someone's already posted a photo of the venue to the group chat. Tom stays at his desk for fifteen minutes pretending to finish something. He puts on his jacket. He could turn left toward The Alchemist or right toward the tram home.

He goes. He always tries to go.

He arrives at 6:15. The group has already colonised a long table near the back. There are cocktails everywhere — bright, elaborate, photogenic. Someone has ordered a round. His colleague spots him: "Tom! What are you having?" It's an innocent question. The most normal question in the world. He hates it.

"Just a tonic water, cheers."

A beat. Not a long one. Maybe half a second. But he feels it. The micro-pause where someone registers that he's not drinking and either decides to ask or decides not to. This time they don't ask. He sits down with his tonic water in a cocktail glass — the bartender was kind enough to make it look like a drink — and joins the conversation.

For the first hour it's fine. Good, even. People are funny, the stories are good, someone's doing an impression of a client that's making everyone cry laughing. Tom's laughing too. This is the part he misses. Not the drinking. This. The communal warmth of a Friday evening with people he likes.

Then hour two hits. The shift. He can feel it before he can name it. The volume goes up. The conversations get looser, louder, more circular. Someone tells the same story twice. Eye contact gets sloppier. People start touching each other's arms. The energy in the room has changed and he hasn't changed with it. He's still at the same frequency he arrived at, and everyone else has tuned to a different station.

A colleague he likes sits down next to him. She's on her third espresso martini. She leans in. "Can I ask you something? Why don't you drink? You don't have to answer." She's being genuine. She's curious, not cruel. But the question lands on him like a spotlight.

He gives the answer he's rehearsed. "Just felt better without it, honestly. Couple of years now." He smiles. She says "that's amazing, good for you" and squeezes his arm and he knows she means it and he also knows that this conversation — this exact conversation — is why he almost didn't come.

Because it's not about her. She was lovely. It's about the fact that in any room where people are drinking, his not-drinking becomes a thing. A talking point. A novelty. An act of quiet rebellion that he didn't sign up for. He just wants to be a person at a table, not a person at a table who doesn't drink.

By 8pm the group is splitting. Half are going to another bar. Someone suggests food. There's a brief negotiation — curry or pizza — that quickly folds because the majority want to keep drinking. Tom says he's going to head off. "Early start tomorrow." Nobody questions it. They never do. That's the thing about being the sober one at the party: you can always leave and nobody notices the gap you leave behind.

He walks to the tram. The evening is warm and the streets are full of people heading out — couples, groups, stag dos. Everyone carrying a drink or heading toward one. He puts his AirPods in.

On the tram he scrolls his phone. Instagram is already full of the evening he just left. Someone's posted the table, the cocktails, the whole scene. He's not in the photo. He was sitting at the end, next to the tonic water.

He gets home. Makes a cheese toastie. Sits on the sofa. The flat is quiet in a way that used to feel like victory — sober Friday night, clear head, no regrets — and now sometimes just feels like a different kind of loss. He didn't lose the hangovers. He lost the after-parties, the late-night conversations, the 2am kebab with someone who becomes a real friend because you were both drunk enough to be honest.

He opens his phone. Types into Google: "social events that aren't about drinking." Gets a sober rave, an alcohol-free festival, and a wellness brunch. All of them feel like they're for a different kind of sober person — the kind who's turned it into an identity. He just wants dinner with people where the absence of alcohol isn't the headline.

He locks his phone. Stares at the ceiling. Thinks: *I'm two years sober and I still haven't figured out where I fit on a Friday night.*

## 5. Emotional Journey

**Trigger**
*I'm walking home from a work thing at 8pm on a Friday. Everyone else went to a second bar. I said I had an early start. I didn't have an early start. I just couldn't do another two hours of holding a tonic water while the room got louder around me. I'm not sad exactly. I'm just tired. Tired of being the odd one out in rooms full of people I actually like.*

**Search / Browse**
*I've looked before. "Sober social events." "Things to do that aren't drinking." I get wellness retreats and alcohol-free raves and sober meet-ups where the first thing everyone talks about is why they stopped drinking. I don't want sobriety to be the activity. I just want to eat dinner with people and not have the drinks menu be the main event.*

**Discovery (sees the ad)**
*I'm scrolling Instagram and something stops me. It's not the usual sober community post. It says something about dinner with 5 strangers, matched on things that actually matter. I almost scroll past. Then I see a line — something about alcohol preference being part of the matching. Not "we're a sober event." Not "alcohol-free night out." Just: we ask if you drink, and we use your answer. I read it again.*

**Consideration**
*I tap through. The sign-up asks real questions. Budget, dietary stuff, what I want more of in my life. And then, buried in there like it's the most normal thing in the world: alcohol preference. Not "are you in recovery?" Not "tell us your sobriety story." Just — do you drink? And if you say no, they match you with other people who said no. That's it. That's the whole thing. I stare at my phone for a full minute because nobody has ever made it that simple.*

**Objections**
*OK but — is it actually groups of non-drinkers? Or is it one sober person sprinkled into a table of drinkers? Because I've been that person. I am that person every Friday. If this is just another table where I'm the odd one out holding a different glass, I'm not interested. I re-read the site. It says non-drinkers are grouped together, matched deliberately based on your answers. The restaurant is chosen to suit. I want to believe this but I've been burned by "we cater to everyone" before. Everyone who says that means "we have one non-alcoholic option."*

**Booking**
*I sign up. The questions take five minutes. I tick "I don't drink." I don't explain why. They don't ask why. That alone is enough to make me trust this slightly. I pick my budget. I say I want more conversation, new perspectives, creativity in my life. I pay. It costs the same as the three tonic waters I nursed at The Alchemist. I close the app and feel something I haven't felt before a social event in a long time: not dread.*

**Waiting**
*Tuesday. I get the confirmation. Restaurant name, address, 7:30pm. I look it up. It's a place I've actually saved on Google Maps — known for good food, interesting cocktail-free menu, not a bar pretending to serve dinner. They chose this place for a reason. I screenshot the confirmation and send it to my sister. She says "that sounds so good, tell me everything after." I pick out a shirt. I haven't picked out a shirt for a social thing in months.*

**Showing up**
*I'm outside the restaurant at 7:25. This is the moment. Because I've been promised something specific — a table where not drinking is normal — and I've been promised things before. I've been told "nobody will care" and then spent the whole evening being cared about in exactly the way I didn't want. If I walk in and someone is ordering a bottle of wine for the table, I'm leaving. I walk in. I find the table. There are two people already seated. Water and a mocktail on the table. No wine list in sight. One of them looks up and says "are you here for the dinner?" and I sit down and nobody asks me what I'm drinking. Nobody asks me what I'm drinking.*

**During dinner**
*There are six of us. All sober — some for years, one for four months, one who's never been a drinker. We don't talk about sobriety for the first forty minutes. We talk about architecture and terrible landlords and someone's theory about why every city has the same three chain restaurants. The food is good. The mocktails are genuinely good — not an afterthought, not a juice with a garnish. At one point someone mentions their sobriety casually, the way you'd mention anything else about yourself, and nobody leans in with concern or admiration. It's just information. I feel something unlock in my chest that I didn't know was locked.*

**After dinner**
*Walking home at 10:15. Same streets as last Friday. Same warm evening, same people heading to bars. But I feel completely different. Not high, not evangelical about it. Just — normal. I just had a normal dinner with normal people and nobody asked me why I wasn't drinking and nobody felt sorry for me and nobody thought I was boring and the evening didn't revolve around alcohol. It revolved around food and conversation and that's all I ever wanted. I text my sister: "It was everything." She sends back seventeen question marks. I'll call her tomorrow. Tonight I just want to sit with this feeling for a bit.*

# QUICK MODE

---

I stopped drinking two years ago and I'm still paying the social tax.

Not the health tax. That's gone. I sleep well, I wake up clear, I run, I read, I'm present in a way I wasn't for most of my twenties. The mornings are mine in a way they never were before. I know this. I'm grateful for it. I'm not here to complain about sobriety. Sobriety gave me my life back.

But it took my social life with it, and nobody warned me about that part.

Here's what nobody tells you: when you stop drinking, you don't just lose the alcohol. You lose the infrastructure. The pub after work. The wine with dinner. The round that turns into three rounds that turns into a late night where you actually talk to someone properly. I know those conversations weren't real — or at least, they weren't sober. But they felt real at the time, and now I have clear-headed evenings where I talk to nobody.

I still go out. I try. I went to a colleague's leaving drinks last Friday at a cocktail bar. I ordered a tonic water. The bartender put it in a cocktail glass, which was kind, I think, or maybe just pity. I sat at the table and for the first hour it was great — funny people, good stories, the kind of energy that makes you glad you came. Then drink two hit. And drink three. And the room shifted. Everyone got louder, warmer, sloppier, more connected. And I stayed the same. I was at the same table but a different event. By 8pm I was functionally alone in a room full of friends.

That's the bit that wears you down. Not the big moments. Not being offered a drink — I can handle that. It's the slow divergence. The way alcohol bonds people in real time and you're watching it happen from the outside. They're all tuning to the same frequency and you're stuck on yours. You haven't changed. But the room has, and you're no longer in it.

And then there's the explaining. God, the explaining. "I don't drink." Two years in and I still brace for the follow-up. Sometimes it's "oh, fair enough." Sometimes it's "not even one?" Sometimes it's the worst one: "good for you, I really respect that." Because now my evening has a theme and the theme is my sobriety and I didn't ask for that. I just wanted to eat dinner.

I've looked at sober social stuff. Alcohol-free raves. Sober brunches. Recovery meet-ups. They're fine for some people. But I don't want sobriety to be the activity. I don't want to bond over the thing we don't do. I want to bond over food, conversation, something funny someone said, the fact that we both think the same obscure film is a masterpiece. I want my sobriety to be a line item, not the headline.

The thing I miss most — and this surprises me — isn't a night out. It's a night in. It's sitting at a restaurant with people, no pressure, good food, conversation that goes somewhere, and nobody performing anything. Nobody performing drunkenness and nobody performing sobriety. Just people being people over a meal.

I'd go out every Wednesday if someone could promise me that. A table where my water is just water. Where the restaurant was chosen because the food is good and the non-alcoholic menu isn't two options at the bottom of the page. Where the other five people at the table either don't drink or don't care, and I never have to find out which because it never comes up.

That's the bar. That's all I want. A dinner where nobody asks why I'm not drinking. Because there's nothing to ask.

Two years sober. Clear head. Good life. I just want somewhere to take it on a Wednesday night.
