## Concept 1: The Frequency Shift

**Insight:** From the day-in-the-life: "Everyone got louder, warmer, sloppier, more connected. And I stayed the same." The room changes at drink two. Everyone tunes to a different frequency and Tom's stuck on his. He hasn't changed — but he's suddenly alone in a room full of friends.

**Emotional lever:** The slow divergence. Not the big dramatic moments of sobriety — the quiet, accumulating isolation of being the only sober person as an evening progresses.

**Angle:** Name the frequency shift directly. Don't talk about drinking or not drinking — talk about the feeling of being left behind in real time. Then offer a table where everyone's on the same frequency all night.

**Headline directions:**
- "You know the moment. Drink two hits and the room changes. You don't."
- "Everyone tunes to a different frequency by 9pm. You're still on yours."
- "You haven't changed. But the room has, and you're no longer in it."

**Format:** Feed ad (the slow build needs text space). Stories/Reels with a subtle visual shift — a table getting louder while one person stays still.

**Test:** Metaphorical ("frequency") vs literal ("everyone gets louder and you stay the same"). Does the metaphor resonate or does plain language hit harder?

## Concept 2: The Question Nobody Should Ask

**Insight:** From the empathy map: "'Can I ask you something? Why don't you drink?'" From quick mode: "'I don't drink.' Two years in and I still brace for the follow-up." The most exhausting part of sobriety isn't not drinking — it's explaining why you don't, every time, to every new person.

**Emotional lever:** The weariness of having to justify a personal choice at every social table. The spotlight nobody asked for.

**Angle:** Call out the question directly. Make the reader feel the weight of being asked again. Then position the product: a table where nobody asks because there's nothing to ask.

**Headline directions:**
- "Nobody asks why you're not drinking. Because nobody's drinking."
- "'Why don't you drink?' — A question you'll never hear at this table."
- "We don't ask why. We just match you with 5 people who also said no."

**Format:** Feed ad (hook is text-driven). Also strong as a Story with a single bold line as the opening frame.

**Test:** Direct address ("Nobody asks why you're not drinking") vs overheard question format ("'Why don't you drink?'"). Does quoting the question make people flinch (good) or feel confrontational?

## Concept 3: We Actually Use Your Answer

**Insight:** From the creative brief: "We ask if you drink and actually use your answer" is the most concrete proof that our matching is real. From matching doc: alcohol is the highest-priority matching factor. Non-drinkers are grouped together, not sprinkled among drinkers.

**Emotional lever:** Surprise. The product does something simple that nobody else does — asks a practical question and acts on it. The surprise is how low the bar is, and that nobody else clears it.

**Angle:** Lead with the differentiator. "We ask if you drink. And then we actually use your answer." Then explain what that means: non-drinkers grouped together, restaurant chosen to suit, mocktail menu that isn't an afterthought. The specificity builds trust.

**Headline directions:**
- "We ask if you drink. And then we actually use your answer."
- "Tick 'I don't drink.' That's it. No explanation, no follow-up. We just match you with people who ticked the same box."
- "One question: Do you drink? Your answer decides who's at your table."

**Format:** Feed ad (needs space for product detail). Carousel (question → answer → what happens next). Reel with a checkbox reveal.

**Test:** Product-led ("we ask if you drink") vs outcome-led ("a table where nobody's drinking"). Does the matching mechanic intrigue people or does the result matter more?

## Concept 4: Sober Isn't the Headline

**Insight:** From the empathy map: "I don't want sobriety to be the activity. I just want to eat dinner with people and not have the drinks menu be the main event." From pain points: sober social spaces make sobriety the whole identity — wellness raves, AF festivals, recovery brunches. He's sober, not a brand.

**Emotional lever:** Relief at not being defined by the one thing he doesn't do. The desire for sobriety to be a line item, not the headline.

**Angle:** Position against sober-identity events. This isn't a sober dinner — it's a dinner where nobody happens to be drinking. The distinction matters enormously to this audience. Don't celebrate their sobriety. Just don't make it a thing.

**Headline directions:**
- "Sober doesn't have to be the most interesting thing about you."
- "Not a sober event. Not a recovery meeting. Just dinner with people where alcohol isn't the point."
- "You don't need another sober brunch. You need dinner where nobody mentions it."

**Format:** Feed ad. Strong as a Reel with a quick-cut "NOT this / NOT this / THIS" format (sober rave → AF brunch → a normal dinner table).

**Test:** Anti-positioning ("not a sober event") vs positive framing ("dinner where nobody mentions it"). Does naming what it ISN'T risk alienating people who do attend sober events?

## Concept 5: The Exit Strategy

**Insight:** From the day-in-the-life: "He says he's going to head off. 'Early start tomorrow.'" From pain points: "Declining invitations and then sitting at home feeling the specific guilt of choosing isolation over discomfort." Tom always has an exit strategy. He calculates when to leave before he arrives.

**Emotional lever:** The exhaustion of planning escape routes for social events. The private negotiation that happens before every invitation gets a yes.

**Angle:** Name the calculation. Every invitation is a risk assessment — how much drinking, can I leave early, will someone ask. Then offer an evening where there's no exit to plan because there's nothing to escape from.

**Headline directions:**
- "Every invitation is a calculation. How much drinking? Can I leave early? Will someone ask?"
- "You always have an exit strategy. What if you didn't need one?"
- "First social event in two years where I didn't calculate an exit time before I arrived."

**Format:** Feed ad. Stories with a text-over-phone visual (the internal monologue before saying yes to plans).

**Test:** The internal monologue ("How much drinking? Can I leave early?") vs the outcome ("First event where I didn't calculate an exit time"). Is the pain or the relief the stronger hook?

## Concept 6: The Late-Night Honest Conversations

**Insight:** From quick mode: "I lost the after-parties, the late-night conversations, the 2am kebab with someone who becomes a real friend because you were both drunk enough to be honest." The thing Tom misses isn't alcohol — it's the intimacy alcohol unlocked. The honest, unguarded conversations that happened at the end of a long night.

**Emotional lever:** Grief for a specific kind of connection. Not romanticising drinking — acknowledging that something real was lost alongside the hangovers.

**Angle:** Name what was actually lost. Not the drink, the conversation. Then offer it back — a dinner where six people are present and clear-headed and honest, not because they're drunk but because the setting makes it safe to be.

**Headline directions:**
- "You didn't lose the drinking. You lost the 2am conversations."
- "The thing you actually miss isn't the drink. It's the honesty that came after the third one."
- "What if the honest conversations didn't need alcohol to start?"

**Format:** Feed ad (the emotional nuance needs space). Reel with a night-walking visual — someone walking home, reflecting on what they gave up and what they got back.

**Test:** Nostalgic ("you lost the 2am conversations") vs forward-looking ("what if they didn't need alcohol?"). Does acknowledging what was lost feel validating or triggering for people in recovery?

## Concept 7: The Glass

**Insight:** From the empathy map: "The moment at a dinner table when the wine arrives and everyone's attention shifts to pouring and tasting and he's just sitting there with his water." From the day-in-the-life: "His tonic water in a cocktail glass — the bartender was kind enough to make it look like a drink."

**Emotional lever:** The visibility of the glass. A water or tonic water at a table of cocktails is a neon sign that invites commentary. The glass is a prop in a performance he never auditioned for.

**Angle:** Make the glass the metaphor. When everyone's holding a cocktail, your water tells a story you didn't want to tell. Then offer a table where your glass is nobody's business — because everyone's glass looks the same.

**Headline directions:**
- "A dinner table where your glass is nobody's business."
- "You know what it's like to hold a tonic water while the wine goes round. What if nobody noticed because nobody cared?"
- "The restaurant has a mocktail menu that isn't an afterthought. Because we chose it for you specifically."

**Format:** Feed ad. Stories/Reels with close-up visuals — glasses on a table, all different, all equal. No wine bottle centrepiece.

**Test:** Metaphorical glass angle ("your glass is nobody's business") vs practical restaurant detail ("mocktail menu that isn't an afterthought"). Does the practical detail build more trust than the emotional angle?

## Concept 8: Wednesday Night, Clear Head

**Insight:** From quick mode: "Two years sober. Clear head. Good life. I just want somewhere to take it on a Wednesday night." From pain points: the loneliness of established sobriety — life is good, mornings are good, but the evenings are empty.

**Emotional lever:** The paradox of having your life together but not having anywhere to take it. Sobriety gave him everything except a social life.

**Angle:** Start from strength. Don't lead with pain — lead with what sobriety gave him. Then name the gap: the evenings. The Wednesday nights. The social spaces that don't exist for someone who's clear-headed and present and just wants to eat dinner with people.

**Headline directions:**
- "Clear head. Good life. Nowhere to take it on a Wednesday night."
- "Sobriety gave you your mornings back. We'll give you your Wednesday nights."
- "You stopped drinking. You didn't stop wanting to go out."

**Format:** Feed ad. Stories with a morning-to-evening visual transition — the good morning, the empty evening, the dinner table that fills the gap.

**Test:** Strength-first ("clear head, good life") vs gap-first ("you stopped drinking, you didn't stop wanting to go out"). Does leading with what they've gained feel empowering or patronising?

## Concept Priority

1. **The Frequency Shift** — The most emotionally specific concept. Names the exact feeling that every non-drinker in a drinking environment recognises. Should be the lead ad because the insight is universal within this audience and deeply underarticulated.

2. **We Actually Use Your Answer** — The strongest differentiator play. This is the product truth that separates Dinner Matcher from every competitor and every sober-social event. "We ask if you drink and actually use your answer" is both a hook and a proof point.

3. **The Question Nobody Should Ask** — Direct, punchy, instantly recognisable. "Nobody asks why you're not drinking. Because nobody's drinking." is the most scroll-stopping single line for this audience.

4. **Sober Isn't the Headline** — Important positioning against sober-identity events. Strong for people who are tired of their sobriety being the most interesting thing about them.

5. **Wednesday Night, Clear Head** — The gentlest entry point. Leads with strength, acknowledges the gap without dwelling on pain. Good for people further along in sobriety who don't need the emotional gut-punch.

6. **The Exit Strategy** — Creates instant solidarity. Everyone who's sober has calculated exit times. Good second-wave ad once the audience is defined.

7. **The Late-Night Honest Conversations** — Emotionally nuanced. Acknowledges what was lost without romanticising drinking. Powerful but needs careful handling — could feel triggering for people in early recovery. Test carefully.

8. **The Glass** — Strong visual concept. Best suited to Stories/Reels where the glass imagery can carry the message. Works better as a supporting creative than a lead.
