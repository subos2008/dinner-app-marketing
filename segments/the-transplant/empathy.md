# DEEP MODE

## 1. Persona Snapshot

I'm Priya, I'm 28. I moved to Manchester from Birmingham nine months ago for a product manager role at a SaaS company in the Northern Quarter. I've got a flatmate called Sophie — found her on SpareRoom. She's perfectly fine but she has her own friends, her own boyfriend, her own life. We share a kitchen but not much else. I know loads of people at work. I just don't know anyone I'd call if I had a really bad day.

- Fantasises about having a "regular thing" — a group she sees weekly without having to orchestrate it
- Worries she's becoming someone who just works and sleeps
- Feels guilty for feeling lonely when she "chose this" — she moved voluntarily, so she should be grateful
- Wonders if she's bad at making friends or if it's just genuinely this hard as an adult
- Sometimes thinks about moving back, then immediately hates herself for thinking it

## 3. Day in the Life — The Triggering Day

It's a Wednesday in late October. Priya's alarm goes off at 7:15. She lies there for a minute, scrolling Instagram before she's even properly awake. Three of her Birmingham friends went for cocktails last night. Jess posted a selfie of the four of them — the usual crew, minus Priya. Nobody tagged her. Why would they? She's not there anymore.

She showers, makes coffee, eats toast standing at the kitchen counter. Sophie's already left. The flat is quiet in a way that used to feel peaceful and now just feels empty.

The commute is fine. She likes the walk through the Northern Quarter — the coffee shops opening up, the guy who sells flowers outside Piccadilly. She puts her AirPods in. She always has her AirPods in now. It occurs to her that she can't remember the last time she had an unplanned conversation with someone who isn't a barista.

Work is busy. She's good at her job, and the morning passes fast. At lunch, three of her colleagues go to the new ramen place together. "Coming, Priya?" She goes. The conversation is fine — sprints, a product launch, someone's holiday photos. She laughs at the right moments. She likes these people. But when lunch ends and they walk back to the office, the feeling that hits her is: that's the most social interaction she'll have today. And it was about work.

At 3pm she has a one-to-one with her manager. He mentions the team Christmas do. "It'll be a good one — we're thinking Escape to Freight Island." She says "sounds great" and means it, but there's a flicker of something underneath: the Christmas do is nice, but it's still just work people. She doesn't have non-work people here.

She finishes at 6. Walks home the long way through Ancoats because the weather's decent. Passes a restaurant with a long table visible through the window — eight or nine people, laughing, clearly mid-celebration. She doesn't stare but she notices. She always notices.

Home. Sophie's out. Again. Not a problem — Sophie has a life, good for her. Priya changes into leggings, considers the gym, decides she doesn't have the energy. She heats up leftover pasta and eats it on the sofa watching something she won't remember the name of tomorrow.

At 8:30 she picks up her phone. Opens Instagram, closes it. Opens WhatsApp — the Birmingham group chat has 47 unread messages. She scrolls through without reading properly, sends a laughing emoji at a random meme. Doesn't type anything real.

She opens Google. Types "things to do in Manchester on your own." Hates that she typed "on your own." Deletes it. Types "social events Manchester." Gets Eventbrite listings: a pub quiz (go alone to a pub quiz?), a pottery class (full), a networking event (absolutely not). Closes the tab.

She opens the Notes app and types nothing. Just stares at the cursor.

At 10pm she's in bed, scrolling TikTok. A video about making friends as an adult comes up. The comments are full of people saying "this is so real" and "I moved cities and it took me two years." Two years. She's nine months in. She screenshots it, doesn't send it to anyone.

She puts her phone down, turns off the light, and thinks: *I like Manchester. I like my job. I just don't have anyone to call about my day.*

Wednesday night. She ate dinner alone.

## 5. Emotional Journey

**Trigger**
*I'm lying on the sofa on a Wednesday night eating reheated pasta and I just watched a table of people through a restaurant window having the kind of evening I want. Something cracks. Not dramatically — I don't cry. I just think: I'm tired of this being my normal.*

**Search / Browse**
*I google "how to meet people Manchester" for the hundredth time. Meetup groups, salsa classes, running clubs. They all require me to show up alone to a room full of strangers with no structure. I'll have to introduce myself and make conversation and hope someone likes me enough to want to see me again. That sounds exhausting. I keep scrolling.*

**Discovery (sees the ad)**
*Something on Instagram stops my thumb. It's not the usual "find your tribe!" cringe. It says something about dinner with 5 strangers, matched on what actually matters. Matched. Not random. There's a line about Wednesday nights. I re-read it. It sounds like someone just... organises a dinner for me? With people who've been selected to be compatible? That's — different.*

**Consideration**
*I tap through. They ask questions when you sign up: do you drink, what do you eat, what's your budget, what do you want more of in your life. These are real questions. Not star signs or personality types. The budget thing is smart — I don't want to end up at a £60-a-head place. And they pick the restaurant. And someone actually reviews the groups before confirming. OK. This is thoughtful.*

**Objections**
*But what if it's weird? What if I'm the only one who's there because they're lonely? What if everyone else already knows each other? What if I have nothing to say? What if — OK, stop. It's dinner. It's one dinner. The worst case is an awkward two hours and a decent meal. I've had worse Tuesdays.*

**Booking**
*I answer the questions. Takes maybe 5 minutes. I'm honest about everything — I say I don't mind about alcohol (I drink but it's not important), I'm vegetarian, mid-range budget. "What do you want more of in your life?" I tick Socialise, Art, and Adventure. I pay. It's done. I feel a small buzz — I've actually done something instead of just thinking about it.*

**Waiting**
*It's Tuesday. I get a confirmation: restaurant name, address, 7:30pm tomorrow. No names of who's coming. Just "your dinner is confirmed." I screenshot it and almost send it to the Birmingham group chat, then don't. I'll tell them after. If it's good. I pick out what I'm going to wear, which is stupid because it's just dinner, but I do it anyway.*

**Showing up**
*I'm standing outside the restaurant at 7:25. My heart is going faster than it should. I almost turn around. Then someone else walks up looking exactly as nervous as I feel and says "Are you here for the...?" and I say "Yeah" and she laughs and says "Thank god, I thought I was going to be the first one." We walk in together.*

**During dinner**
*There are six of us. Two women, three men, one non-binary person. Everyone's around my age. The questions on the table help — within 20 minutes we're past the small talk and someone's telling a story about the worst flat viewing they've ever had and I'm laughing properly for the first time in weeks. Nobody asks "so what do you do?" for the first 40 minutes. I learn that two of them also moved here recently. I'm not the only one.*

**After dinner**
*Walking home at 10:30, I feel lighter than I have in months. Not because I've found my best friends — I might never see these people again. But because the evening proved something: I can still do this. I can sit at a table with strangers and be myself and it works. I take out my phone and text the Birmingham group chat: "Just had dinner with 5 strangers through this app and it was actually amazing." Jess replies: "WHAT. Tell me everything."*

# QUICK MODE

---

I moved here nine months ago and I still don't know how to answer the question "what are you doing this weekend?" honestly. The honest answer is nothing. Or, nothing with other people. I'll go to the gym, I'll go to that coffee place on Tib Street, I'll walk around Ancoats pretending I'm exploring when actually I've walked this route forty times. I'll do laundry. I'll probably cook something nice because cooking for one is my way of pretending this is a lifestyle choice and not just what happens when you don't have anyone to eat with.

The thing nobody tells you about moving cities is that it's not the big moments that get you. It's not Christmas or your birthday. It's a random Wednesday. It's reheating pasta and noticing the flat is so quiet you can hear the fridge humming. It's seeing a group through a restaurant window and feeling this specific kind of envy that isn't about money or success — it's about belonging. They belong to each other. I don't belong to anyone here yet.

I have people. I have work people. We go to lunch and talk about product roadmaps and say "we should hang out sometime" and we never do. I have Sophie, my flatmate, who is genuinely lovely and also has a full life that I'm not part of. I have my Birmingham friends on a group chat that I'm slowly becoming a ghost in. I send laughing emojis. I don't say real things anymore because real things require context and context requires presence and I'm not present in their lives anymore. I'm a name on a screen.

I downloaded Bumble BFF once. Swiped through a few profiles. It felt like applying for friendship. "Hi! I love brunch and dogs and I'm looking for my partner in crime!" I closed the app and deleted it and felt worse than before I opened it. I don't want to audition to be someone's friend. I want to sit at a table and talk about real things and not have to perform.

Sometimes I think I'm being dramatic. Millions of people move cities. They manage. They join running clubs and make friends at yoga and build these networks. Why can't I? Am I broken? Am I just bad at this? And then I talk to someone at work who also moved here recently and they say the exact same things and I realise: no, it's just genuinely this hard. Making friends as an adult is brutal and nobody prepares you for it.

The worst part is the gap. The gap between the life I show people and the life I actually live. On Instagram I'm thriving — the Northern Quarter, a flat white, some street art, "Manchester, you beauty." In reality I haven't had a proper conversation with someone who isn't a colleague or a barista in two weeks. In reality I screenshot TikToks about loneliness and don't send them to anyone because who would I send them to?

What I actually want is embarrassingly simple. I want a table. I want to sit at a table with a few people on a Wednesday night and talk about something that isn't work. I want someone to say "same time next week?" and mean it. I want plans that I didn't have to beg for or manufacture or pretend are more casual than they are. I want to text someone "you'll never guess what happened today" and have them care.

I don't need a best friend. I just need proof that this city has space for me. That I didn't leave everything behind for a nice flat and an empty calendar.
