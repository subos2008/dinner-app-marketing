# DEEP MODE

## 1. Persona Snapshot

I'm Jess, I'm 31. I live in Chorlton, Manchester, in a two-bed flat above a vintage shop. I work three days a week as a massage therapist and spend the rest of my time between yoga classes, volunteering at a community garden, and whatever workshop or retreat catches my attention. Last month it was a three-day silent retreat in the Lake District. This month it's a breathwork course in Ancoats every Tuesday evening. I stopped drinking two years ago — not dramatically, not because of a problem, just because it stopped aligning with how I wanted to feel. I meditate every morning. I journal. I go to sound baths. I'm happier and healthier than I've ever been.

And I'm lonelier than I've ever been.

My old friends — the ones from uni, from my twenties — they're lovely. They just still live in the pub. Friday night is drinks. Saturday is brunch with bottomless prosecco. Sunday is recovery. I've done the cycle a hundred times and I just don't want to anymore. But when I opted out of the drinking, I accidentally opted out of the social life. Nobody excluded me. I excluded myself. And now I don't know where I fit.

## 3. Day in the Life — The Triggering Day

It's a Wednesday in November. Jess wakes at 6:15 without an alarm — she's been doing that for months, her body clock trained by the morning routine. She sits up, crosses her legs on the bed, closes her eyes. Twenty minutes of meditation. Some days it's deep and clear. Today it's fidgety. She sits with it anyway.

She makes a smoothie. Turmeric, ginger, banana, oat milk, a scoop of something adaptogenic she bought at the health store. She drinks it standing at the kitchen window, looking at the grey November sky over Chorlton. It's not a beautiful morning but she notices it.

She teaches two massage clients back-to-back in the morning. Between sessions she eats an energy ball and checks her phone. There's a message in the old uni group chat — everyone went to a new bar in the Northern Quarter on Saturday. Photos of cocktails, someone doing a shot, a selfie where they all look happy and drunk. She types "looks fun!" and puts the phone down. She wasn't invited. Or she was, and she said no so many times they stopped asking. She's not sure which.

After her last client, she drives to a yoga class in Didsbury. It's good — strong vinyasa, good teacher, the kind where she's out of her head for an hour. Afterwards she rolls up her mat and looks around. Twenty people in that room. She said "excuse me" to one of them. That was her only interaction.

She gets home at 4pm and opens her laptop. There's a breathwork circle tonight in the Northern Quarter — she'd seen it last week. Seven o'clock. She checks the details: £15, small group, guided by a facilitator she follows on Instagram. She books it immediately. The buzz comes back — the one she gets when there's something on the calendar that aligns with who she is.

She eats early. A big salad with roasted sweet potato and tahini. Alone, at the kitchen table, listening to a podcast about consciousness that she'd never be able to describe to anyone she knows without sounding unhinged.

The breathwork session is beautiful. Ninety minutes of deep, intentional breathing. She cries at one point — not from sadness, from release. Afterwards the group sits in a circle and people share. A woman called Maya talks about feeling disconnected from her friends since she stopped drinking. Jess nods. A man called Tom says he's been sober for a year and the hardest part isn't the not-drinking, it's the not-belonging. Jess feels something unlock in her chest. These people get it.

They finish at 8:30. Everyone puts on coats. Maya says "that was so lovely" and Jess says "it really was" and they smile and Maya walks one way and Jess walks the other.

That's it.

No one suggests a tea. No one swaps numbers. They shared something real and deep for ninety minutes and then dispersed back into their separate lives. By the time Jess is in her car, the connection is already fading. She drives home through the dark streets and pulls into her parking spot and sits there for a minute with the engine off, staring at nothing.

She changed her life. She did the work. She meditates, she eats well, she doesn't drink, she goes to things that light her up. She's the healthiest and most self-aware she's ever been.

And she's going to walk into her flat and spend the rest of the evening alone.

She opens her phone. Scrolls Instagram. Someone she vaguely knows has posted a group dinner photo — eight women around a table, wine glasses, candles, everyone mid-laugh. She stares at it. Not at the wine. At the table. At the togetherness. At the Wednesday night that she wants and doesn't have.

She puts the phone down and goes inside.

## 5. Emotional Journey

**Trigger**
*I'm driving home from a breathwork circle on a Wednesday night. I've just spent ninety minutes breathing, crying, connecting with strangers who said things I've been thinking for months. And now I'm in a dark car park, alone, and the feeling is already slipping away. It hits me: I can find these moments, but I can't keep them. The people who get me exist — I just never see them again.*

**Search / Browse**
*I know what's out there. More workshops. More circles. More retreats where I feel alive for 48 hours and then re-enter my real life. I want something simpler. I want a dinner. A regular, normal dinner with people who don't drink, who eat with intention, who talk about real things. Not another ceremony. Just a table. But where do you find that? Meetup has pub crawls and business networking. Eventbrite has alcohol-free raves (not what I'm after). I look at a few sober social events — they're framed around recovery, and I'm not recovering from anything. I just don't drink.*

**Discovery (sees the ad)**
*Something on Instagram catches me. It's not a wellness event. It's not "raise your vibration." It says something about dinner with 5 strangers, matched on what actually matters. Matched. The word sticks. I read on. They ask about alcohol — and if you don't drink, you're matched with other people who don't drink. Every person at the table. Not just you politely nursing a water while everyone else orders wine. Everyone.*

**Consideration**
*I tap through. The sign-up asks about drinking — yes, no, don't mind. Diet. Budget. And then: "What do you want more of in your life?" The options include meditation, outdoors, sober events. These are my words. I keep reading. They pick the restaurant. Every group is matched deliberately based on your answers. It's every Wednesday. I feel something — not excitement exactly. Relief. Somebody built the thing I've been wishing existed.*

**Objections**
*But what if the people are boring? What if they're sober but corporate? What if it's just another awkward social thing where I'm performing normality? What if "matched" just means random? And then I think: the worst case is a mediocre dinner with people who at least don't drink. That's already better than sitting alone in my flat. And the best case — the best case is a table where I don't have to explain myself.*

**Booking**
*I answer the questions. No, I don't drink. Plant-based. Mid-range budget. "What do you want more of?" I tick meditation, outdoors, sober events. I add "conscious connection" in the freetext box because of course I do. I pay. I feel calm. Not buzzy. Calm. Like I've finally done something that matches who I actually am.*

**Waiting**
*Tuesday. Restaurant confirmed. No names. I look up the restaurant — it's a plant-based place in the Northern Quarter with good reviews. The fact they picked a plant-based restaurant because the group needed one makes me trust this more. I tell no one. My friends would think it's weird. My mum would worry. I'll just go.*

**Showing up**
*I arrive at 7:25. There's a woman outside looking at her phone, and when she sees me she says "Come Join Us?" and I say yes and she laughs and says "I was scared I'd be the only one." We walk in together. The table is set for six. Candles. Water, no wine list. Already different.*

**During dinner**
*There are six of us. The first thing I notice: nobody orders alcohol. Nobody even mentions it. It's just not part of the evening. The icebreaker questions help — within fifteen minutes someone mentions they've just come back from a retreat and two others lean in. I say I do breathwork and nobody's eyes glaze over. Someone asks me what kind. We talk about morning routines, about purpose, about what we want to build. It's the conversation I have at retreats, but at a restaurant, on a Wednesday night, with food I'd actually choose to eat. One person says "I've been looking for this exact thing" and the table goes quiet for a moment because we all have.*

**After dinner**
*Walking home at 10:15, I feel full — not just from the food. From the conversation. From the ease of it. From the fact that for two and a half hours, I didn't have to translate my life into something palatable for people who don't get it. I was just myself, at a table, with five people who live the same way I do. I didn't have to explain why I don't drink. I didn't have to downplay the meditation. I didn't have to be less. I open my phone and book next Wednesday before I've even unlocked my front door.*

# QUICK MODE

I've done so much work on myself in the last two years and I don't know how to talk about it without sounding like a wanker. I meditate every morning. I do breathwork. I've been sober for two years. I eat plants. I go to retreats and workshops and sound baths and I come home buzzing with this feeling of connection and expansion and then I walk into my flat and it's just me and the feeling starts leaking out before I've even taken my shoes off.

The thing is, I know people who get it. I meet them at workshops. I sit next to them at breathwork circles. We share something genuinely real for an hour or two — someone cries, someone laughs, someone says the exact thing you've been thinking — and then the facilitator says namaste and everyone puts on their coats and goes home and I never see them again. Connection on a timer. Deep and then gone.

My old friends think I've gone a bit off the deep end. They don't say it like that. They say "you've changed" in this voice that's supposed to be neutral but isn't. They're still going to the pub every Friday and I love them but I can't do it anymore. I sit there with my lime and soda watching them get progressively louder and looser and I'm just present enough to notice how not-present everyone else is. I leave at 9:30. I drive home. I feel like a visitor in my own social life.

It's not that I'm better than them. I have to keep saying that because I'm scared it's what they think. It's not that. It's just that my life has gone in a direction that none of my existing relationships can follow. I want to talk about meaning and purpose and what we're actually doing here and the best I get is "that sounds really cool" and then someone changes the subject to what happened on Saturday night.

The loneliest part is the gap. Between who I am at a retreat and who I am on a Tuesday evening. Between the morning meditation where everything feels clear and connected and the 7pm reality of eating tahini sweet potato alone at the kitchen table while listening to a consciousness podcast that I couldn't describe to a single person I know without them looking at me like I've joined a cult.

What I actually want is embarrassingly normal. I want to sit at a table on a Wednesday night with people who don't drink, who eat real food, who meditate or do yoga or whatever their thing is — and just have dinner. Not a ceremony. Not a circle. Not a facilitated sharing experience. Just dinner. Conversation that goes past surface level because the people at the table have already done the work of going past surface level in their own lives.

I want to say "I went to a breathwork session" and nobody's eyes glaze over. I want the sober thing to not be a thing — not something I explain, not something I defend, just the ambient default of the evening. I want to be asked "what do you want more of in your life?" and have it be a real question, not a motivational poster.

I want a table. My table. With people who've changed their lives too and are looking for the same thing I am: someone to have dinner with who actually gets it.
