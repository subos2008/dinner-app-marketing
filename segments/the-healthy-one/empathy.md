# DEEP MODE

## 1. Persona Snapshot

I'm Tom, I'm 31. I moved to Manchester two years ago — that part's settled. I've got a decent group of mates from work and five-a-side. The thing that isn't settled is my body. Eight months ago I decided enough was enough. I was 16 stone, out of breath walking up stairs, and my GP said my blood pressure was "worth watching." I started tracking calories on MyFitnessPal, going to the gym four times a week, meal prepping on Sundays. I've lost two stone. I feel better than I have in years. But somewhere in the process, I lost my social life too. Every dinner out is a negotiation. Every pub trip is a willpower test. My mates don't mean to make it hard — they just don't think about it. "Let's get pizza." "Go on, have a pint." "One dessert won't kill you." I'm tired of being the difficult one at the table.

## 2. Empathy Map

### Think & Feel

- "I'm making real progress and nobody around me seems to care about protecting it."
- Proud of the discipline but secretly worried it's turning him into someone boring — the guy who can't just relax and eat
- Calculates macros in his head before every meal out and hates that he does it
- Feels a low-level resentment toward friends who can eat whatever they want and not think about it
- Wonders whether he'll ever be able to eat out without it being a mental event
- Caught between wanting to be "normal" at dinner and wanting to stay on track
- Scared the weight will come back the moment he loosens up — one pizza and the whole thing unravels
- Sometimes thinks: "Is being healthy supposed to be this lonely?"

### Say & Do

- **To mates suggesting dinner:** "Yeah sounds good — where are we going?" (Then immediately checks the menu online to see if there's anything he can eat)
- **At the table:** Orders the grilled chicken or the salmon salad while everyone else orders burgers. Says "nah, I'm good" to the shared starter. Pretends he doesn't notice the eye-rolls.
- **To a colleague offering cake:** "I'm alright, cheers." Doesn't explain why. Has explained too many times before.
- **On his phone:** Scrolls r/loseit, watches fitness YouTube, tracks every meal in MyFitnessPal. His screen time on health apps is 45 minutes a day.
- **On Instagram:** Follows food prep accounts, gym transformation pages, Joe Wicks. Doesn't post about his own journey — doesn't want to be "that guy."
- **On a Friday night:** Stays home more often than he used to. Easier to cook something he's planned than to fight a restaurant menu. Tells himself it's discipline, but it feels like isolation.
- Turns down dinner invitations roughly once a fortnight — not because he doesn't want to go, but because the restaurant is a burger place or a pizza joint and he can't face scanning the menu for the one thing he can eat

### See

- Friends ordering without thinking — a burger, chips, a beer — while he's doing maths in his head
- Instagram full of before-and-afters, "what I eat in a day" content, gym progress photos — a world that understands him but only exists online
- Restaurant menus that are 80% things he can't eat and 20% sad salads
- His meal prep containers lined up in the fridge — neat, efficient, and lonely
- Colleagues eating biscuits in meetings without a second thought
- Group dinner photos on social media where everyone looks happy and relaxed and nobody is stressing about the menu
- The gym at 6am — full of people on the same journey, none of them talking to each other

### Hear

- "Go on, live a little." (From friends who don't understand what it took to get here.)
- "You've lost loads, you can afford a cheat day." (He can't, not mentally.)
- "What CAN you eat?" (Said with slight impatience when choosing a restaurant.)
- "You're no fun anymore." (Half-joking, fully felt.)
- "I wish I had your discipline." (Said admiringly by someone who then orders dessert. It's not admiration. It's distance.)
- His PT saying "consistency is everything" and meaning food, not just the gym
- The silence when he says "I'll just have a salad" and everyone else is three courses deep
- His own internal voice: "Just eat the burger. You're making this weird. You're ruining the vibe."

## 3. Day in the Life — The Triggering Day

It's a Saturday in November. Tom wakes at 7:30 without an alarm — his body's adjusted to early mornings since he started training. He lies in bed for a few minutes scrolling through Instagram. A transformation post: someone who lost 30kg. He double-taps it, feels a flash of solidarity, then keeps scrolling. His mate Dan has posted a photo from last night — five of them at Rudy's, the Neapolitan pizza place in Ancoats. They look happy. Tom wasn't there. He said he was tired, which was technically true and completely beside the point. The real reason is that Rudy's is a pizza restaurant and he didn't want to sit there eating a side salad while everyone else had the thing the restaurant is famous for.

He gets up, makes his usual breakfast: eggs, turkey bacon, spinach, black coffee. Logs it in MyFitnessPal. 387 calories, 42g protein. He feels good about this. He also feels like someone who logs breakfast on a Saturday morning is probably not living their best life.

The gym is busy. He does an upper body session, hits a PB on bench press, and for 90 minutes everything makes sense. He's in a room full of people who get it — who understand that discipline has a cost and the cost is worth it. Nobody here is offering him cake or asking why he won't have a beer. But nobody here is talking to him either. He puts his headphones on, does his sets, leaves.

He showers, meal preps for the week. Chicken thighs, brown rice, roasted veg. Four containers. He puts them in the fridge next to the leftovers from last night's dinner — grilled salmon and sweet potato, eaten alone at his kitchen counter while watching the Premier League.

At 2pm, his phone buzzes. Group chat: "Curry tonight? Asha's at 8?" Tom likes the message. Then opens the Asha's menu on his phone. He scrolls through it looking for something that works. Tandoori chicken could be OK, but the sides — naan, rice, everything comes loaded with ghee. He could ask for a dry tandoori with a side of plain rice, but he knows how that plays out. He'll order it, someone will say "just get a korma, live a bit," and he'll either cave or explain himself again and feel like the killjoy.

He types "yeah maybe, might be tired though" and puts the phone down. He already knows he's not going. He'll heat up one of the meal prep containers instead. He tells himself this is the smart choice. His body tells him one thing; the rest of him tells him another.

At 8pm he's on the sofa. He's eaten his chicken and rice. It was fine. Nutritionally optimal. The group chat is pinging — they're at Asha's, someone's already ordered poppadoms for the table, someone else is three pints deep. Tom watches it scroll. He doesn't reply.

He opens Reddit. Scrolls r/loseit. Someone's posted about losing friends since changing their diet. The comments are full of people saying the same thing: "My mates don't get it." "I eat alone most of the time now." "It's like I traded a social life for abs." Tom reads every comment. Doesn't post anything. He doesn't need advice. He just needs to know it's not just him.

At 10pm he's in bed. He checks his macro tracker — hit his protein target, stayed under his calorie limit. A successful day by every metric except the one that matters: he spent the whole thing alone. Again. Not because he doesn't have friends. Because eating with them has become harder than eating without them.

Saturday night. He ate dinner alone. Because it was easier.

## 5. Emotional Journey

**Trigger**
*It's Saturday night and I'm eating meal-prepped chicken at my kitchen counter while the group chat blows up with photos from Asha's. I didn't go because I couldn't face the menu. I hit my protein target today. I also spoke to nobody outside the gym. This isn't what progress is supposed to feel like.*

**Search / Browse**
*I don't even know what to google. "Healthy restaurants Manchester"? I know restaurants. That's not the problem. The problem is going with people who make me feel like a freak for ordering carefully. I scroll Reddit for a bit, read some threads about socialising on a diet. Lots of "I feel this" comments. No solutions. I check a few fitness meetup groups — they're all running clubs and boot camps. I don't want to exercise with strangers. I want to eat with them.*

**Discovery (sees the ad)**
*I'm on Instagram, halfway through a reel about meal prep, and something stops me. It's not a gym ad. It's an ad about dinner. "A dinner where nobody judges what's on your plate." I re-read it. It mentions matching based on what you eat and your budget. Matching. By diet. So I wouldn't be the only one at the table ordering grilled chicken?*

**Consideration**
*I tap through. They ask what you eat — omnivore, flexitarian, vegetarian, vegan, carnivore. And your budget. And whether you drink. And then they match you with 5 other people based on your answers. So if I say I'm watching what I eat, I get sat with other people who are watching what they eat? At a restaurant they've picked? Someone's actually thought about this. The restaurant is curated — 4.3+ Google rating, matched to the group. This isn't a sad salad bar. This is a real dinner with people who eat like I do.*

**Objections**
*But what if it's all people who want to talk about macros and creatine for two hours? What if it's weird? What if the restaurant is still terrible for what I eat? ... OK but they literally match the restaurant to the group's dietary preferences. And it's one dinner. Worst case, I eat a decent meal and make awkward small talk for two hours. I've had worse Saturdays — specifically, today.*

**Booking**
*I fill in the questions. I say I drink but not much, I'm an omnivore (just a careful one), mid-range budget. "What do you want more of in your life?" I tick Gym, Socialise, and Outdoors. I pay. It takes five minutes. I feel a weird mix of relief and nerves. I've actually signed up for a dinner I don't have to negotiate.*

**Waiting**
*It's Tuesday. I get a message: restaurant name, address, 7:30pm tomorrow. I google the restaurant immediately. The menu has grilled options, protein-heavy dishes, actual variety. It's not a burger joint. I don't have to strategise. I can just... pick something and eat it. I haven't felt like that about a restaurant in months.*

**Showing up**
*I'm outside at 7:25, which is too early, so I walk around the block once. When I come back there's a woman standing near the door looking at her phone. "Dinner Matcher?" she says. "Yeah." She laughs. "I nearly didn't come. But I figured one dinner is one dinner." We walk in together. I already feel less ridiculous.*

**During dinner**
*There are six of us. Everyone orders like I do — considered, intentional, not ashamed. One person's doing keto, two are calorie-counting, one's flexitarian, one just "tries to eat clean." Nobody orders a burger and chips. Nobody says "go on, live a little." The conversation starts with food — someone asks what app I use for tracking — but within 20 minutes we're talking about work, about the gym, about moving to Manchester, about whether anyone's hiked Kinder Scout. I eat grilled sea bass and vegetables. It's good. Nobody looks at my plate.*

**After dinner**
*I walk home at 10:15 and something's different. I'm not doing the thing I usually do after eating out, which is checking MyFitnessPal and calculating the damage. There's no damage. I ate well, I enjoyed it, and I spent an evening with people who didn't make me feel like a weirdo for caring about food. I open the group chat — the one that was at Asha's last night. I type: "Had dinner with 5 strangers through this app. All health-conscious. Nobody judged my order. Actually decent." Dan replies: "Mate that sounds class. What app?"*

# QUICK MODE

---

I never thought getting healthy would make me lonely. That's the bit nobody warns you about. You see the transformation posts, the before-and-afters, the "trust the process" captions. They show you the body. They don't show you the Saturday nights.

Eight months in. I've lost two stone. My clothes fit different. I sleep better. I can run for the bus without wanting to die. By every measurable standard, I'm in the best shape of my life. And I eat alone almost every night.

It's not that I don't have friends. I have plenty. Dan, Gaz, the five-a-side lot. They're good people. They just don't think about food the way I have to. "Let's get pizza" — they say it the way normal people say it, casually, without calculating the macros on a margherita in their head before they've even replied to the message. For them it's just dinner. For me it's an event. Can I eat there? What's on the menu? If I go, can I stay on track? If I say no, is this the third dinner I've skipped this month? Am I becoming the guy who never comes out?

I am becoming the guy who never comes out. Not because I'm antisocial. Because eating out is exhausting when you're watching what you eat. The menus are designed for people who aren't counting anything. Everything's loaded with stuff I'm trying to avoid. And I could just order the grilled chicken and a side salad, but then someone says "is that all you're having?" and I have to choose between lying ("yeah I'm not that hungry") and explaining ("actually I'm tracking my calories and that chicken breast is about 350 cals which leaves room for—") and both options make me feel like a freak.

The worst part is the "live a little" crowd. They mean well. They genuinely think they're being kind. "Go on, have a dessert." "One pint won't kill you." "You've lost loads, you can afford it." They don't understand that it's not about one dessert. It's about the fact that every group meal is a willpower test and I'm tired of being tested. I'm tired of being the difficult one. I'm tired of doing maths while everyone else just eats.

So I stay home. I meal prep on Sundays. Chicken, rice, veg, containers in the fridge, everything measured and logged. It's efficient. It's effective. It's exactly as depressing as it sounds when you eat it alone on a Saturday night watching Match of the Day while the group chat pings with photos from a restaurant you chose not to go to.

The fitness community online gets it. Reddit threads, YouTube comments, people who've been where I am. They understand the trade-off. But it's all screens. It's not a table. It's not someone across from me eating the same way I eat and not making a thing of it.

What I actually want is stupid simple. I want a dinner out — a proper one, at a decent restaurant — where I don't have to fight the menu and nobody makes me feel weird for ordering carefully. I want to sit with people who eat like I do. Not to talk about macros all night, just to eat like normal humans who happen to give a shit about what goes into their body. I want to say yes to a dinner invitation and not feel my stomach drop when I see the restaurant name.

I didn't lose two stone to sit at home. I lost it so I could live better. And right now, "better" doesn't include other people. That's the part I need to fix.
