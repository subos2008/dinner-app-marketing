# DEEP MODE

## 1. Persona Snapshot

I'm Brendan, I'm 34. I work remotely as a data analyst for a logistics company based in Leeds. I live alone in a one-bedroom flat in Chorlton. I've been in Manchester my whole life — I didn't move here, I didn't lose a friend group to a breakup or a relocation. I just never really built one. I had a few mates from school but those drifted when people paired off and had kids. I'm not depressed, not in crisis. I just eat every meal alone and I have for a long time, and recently I've started noticing it.

## 2. Empathy Map

### Think & Feel

- "I should be used to this by now. I am used to it. That's the problem."
- A low-level awareness that his social life isn't normal, offset by years of telling himself he's just independent
- Quietly envious of people who seem to accumulate friends effortlessly — not bitterly, more like watching someone do a skill he never learned
- Thinks about reaching out to old school mates sometimes, writes the message in his head, never sends it — the gap is too wide now
- Wonders if he missed some developmental window for learning how to make friends, like a language you can only pick up as a child
- Feels a specific dread about weekends: two days of unstructured time with no one expecting anything from him
- Sometimes feels invisible — not overlooked, just genuinely unseen, like he could vanish and it would take days for anyone to notice
- Has made peace with being alone but hasn't made peace with the idea this might be permanent

### Say & Do

- **At work (Slack):** Professional, helpful, a bit dry. People think he's quiet but competent. He's never been on a video call with his camera on voluntarily.
- **To the one colleague he's friendly with:** "Yeah, quiet weekend. Caught up on some stuff." ('Stuff' was eight hours of YouTube and a walk to Tesco.)
- **At the supermarket:** Buys single portions. Has never once bought a sharing bag of anything and actually shared it.
- **On the phone to his mum (Sundays, 4pm, like clockwork):** "Yeah, I'm fine, Mum. Yeah. No, I did go out actually." (He went to the corner shop.)
- Runs alone three times a week, same route along the Mersey, same podcast. Never joined a running club because the idea of introducing himself to a group makes his chest tight.
- Has a Reddit account with 14,000 karma. That's where his social energy goes. Strangers on the internet are easier.
- Cooks proper meals — not sad meals, genuinely good food. Roast dinners on Sundays for one. It's a point of pride that doubles as a point of pain.
- Goes to the cinema alone on Tuesday afternoons because it's quieter and nobody sees him there alone

### See

- Couples at Aldi doing a weekly shop together, negotiating what to have for dinner — a mundane interaction he has literally never had
- Neighbours who wave but have never learned his name despite three years on the same corridor
- Group chats on his phone: one work channel, one family channel. That's it. No friendship groups. No banter threads.
- Restaurant tables set for two or four, rarely for one — even the furniture assumes you have someone
- Social media posts from acquaintances at weddings, stag dos, holidays together — evidence of lives built around other people
- His own flat, which is clean and organised and contains no evidence that anyone else has ever been in it
- The group run passing his window Saturday mornings — twelve people chatting, breath visible in the cold, connected

### Hear

- "You're so independent!" (Said admiringly by his mum. Feels like a misdiagnosis.)
- "We should get a beer sometime." (From the one work mate. Neither of them has ever followed up.)
- Podcasts. Hours of podcasts. Other people's conversations filling his flat like background noise.
- The specific silence of eating a meal with no TV, no podcast, no distraction — just the sound of his own chewing
- "Are you seeing anyone?" (From his sister, every few months. He's not. He also isn't seeing anyone platonically.)
- "You just need to put yourself out there." (The single most unhelpful sentence in the English language.)
- The notification sounds from apps that are never social — banking, news, delivery updates. His phone never buzzes with a person.

## 3. Day in the Life — The Triggering Day

It's a Saturday in early March. Brendan wakes up at 8:20 without an alarm. He lies in bed for a while, not scrolling, not thinking about anything specific. Just lying there. The flat is quiet. It's always quiet.

He gets up, makes coffee. French press, properly done — he watched a James Hoffmann video about grind size last month and now he's particular about it. He stands at the kitchen window while it brews and looks at the street below. A couple walks past with a dog. A man jogs by in a high-vis vest. A woman pushes a pram. Everyone is mid-story. He's watching from above.

Breakfast is eggs on toast. He eats at the small table by the window, which seats two. Nobody has ever sat in the other chair. He bought the table because a single-seat table felt too on the nose.

He opens his laptop. He's not working — it's Saturday — but the laptop is the portal to everything. Reddit for a bit. A thread about meal prepping. A thread about the best walks near Manchester. He bookmarks the walks thread. He's bookmarked about forty walks. He's done three of them.

At 10:30 he goes for his run. The usual route, the usual podcast — a football tactics show. He runs well. He's fit, actually. Slim, consistent, capable. He passes a group of five or six people stretching near the bridge. One of them says "morning!" as he goes past and he says "morning" back and it's the first word he's spoken aloud today.

He showers. Gets dressed. Jeans, a decent jumper. He has no plans but he dresses like someone who might. He drives to the Trafford Centre because he needs new trainers, but if he's honest the real reason is that it's a place to be around people without having to interact with them. He walks through the mall, tries on shoes, makes a purchase. The person at the till says "doing anything nice this weekend?" and he says "not much, just this" and smiles and moves on and in the car park he sits in his car for a moment because the question landed somewhere he wasn't expecting.

He drives home. Makes lunch — a proper sandwich, good bread, the works. Eats it on the sofa watching a documentary about deep-sea exploration. The documentary is interesting. He has a thought about it, the kind of thought you'd normally say out loud — "that's mad, how do they even film that?" — but there's nobody to say it to, so the thought just evaporates.

At 3pm the flat gets very still. This is the part of Saturday he likes least. The morning has structure — coffee, run, errand. The evening has permission to do nothing because it's evening. But 3pm on a Saturday is the hour where the absence is loudest. He's not sad. He's not spiralling. He's just sitting in a quiet flat with nothing ahead of him and no one expecting him anywhere, and the feeling isn't dramatic, it's just flat. A low hum of nothing.

He picks up his phone. Opens Instagram, which he barely uses. An old school friend has posted a photo from a dinner out — six of them around a table, the remains of plates and wine glasses, someone mid-laugh. The caption is "monthly dinner crew, going strong." Monthly dinner crew. They have a name for it. They have a rhythm. It's so casual for them that they've made it a tradition without even trying.

He double-taps the photo and puts his phone down.

He cooks in the evening. A chicken thigh thing with roasted vegetables. He's good at this. He plates it properly, puts on some music. Eats at the two-person table. The food is excellent. Nobody will ever know.

At 9pm he's on the sofa. He opens Reddit and starts typing a post in r/CasualUK. "What do people actually do on weekends?" He writes three paragraphs, reads them back, deletes the whole thing. Too revealing. He closes the app.

He picks up his phone again. Opens Google. Doesn't type anything for a moment. Then: "dinner with strangers Manchester." He doesn't know what he's looking for. He's not even sure he'd do anything about it. He just knows that the image of six people around a table has been sitting in his head all afternoon, and not because of the food.

He reads a few results. Closes the phone. Brushes his teeth. Gets into bed. Lies in the dark.

The flat is quiet. It's always quiet.

## 5. Emotional Journey

**Trigger**
*It's 3pm on a Saturday and I'm sitting in my flat and a school friend has posted a photo of six people at a table with the caption "monthly dinner crew." And the thing that gets me isn't envy. It's how easy it looks. They just... have that. I don't have that. I've never had that. And today, for some reason, the fact I don't have that isn't something I can just file away. It sits there.*

**Search / Browse**
*I don't really search. I'm not a joiner and I know it. I half-type things into Google and delete them. "How to meet people." Delete. "Social events Manchester." Delete. I don't delete because the results are bad. I delete because every result requires me to do the one thing I can't do: walk into a room full of strangers and make myself known. Running club. Board game night. Language exchange. They all assume a baseline confidence I don't have. I close the laptop.*

**Discovery (sees the ad)**
*It's three days later, Tuesday night, scrolling Instagram. An ad. Simple. Something like: "You don't have to be a social person to want connection." I stop. Not because of the design or the branding. Because of the word "have." You don't HAVE to be. Like it's granting permission. I read the rest. Dinner with 5 matched strangers. Every Wednesday. Restaurant chosen for you. I don't tap through. Not yet. But I screenshot it. That's significant. I don't screenshot things.*

**Consideration**
*I go back to the screenshot on Thursday. Tap through this time. The sign-up asks questions: do you drink, dietary requirements, budget range, age. "What do you want more of in your life?" I read the options. Sports. Art. Business. Meditation. Socialise. The word "socialise" shouldn't make me feel something, but it does. Then I read that every group is matched deliberately based on your answers. Not an algorithm guessing. Matched specifically, on purpose, because someone gives a shit about whether this is good. I close the tab but I don't close the thought.*

**Objections**
*This is where I live for a week. I think about it on my run. I think about it while cooking. I think about it at 3pm on the next Saturday when the flat goes quiet again. The objections come in waves. What if I'm the awkward one. What if everyone else is more social than me and I'm the charity case. What if they can tell I haven't had a proper conversation with someone in weeks. What if I sit there and can't think of anything to say. What if they're all friends by the end and I'm still on the outside. What if this is just another thing I try that confirms what I already suspect about myself. The objections aren't rational. I know that. But they're loud. I open the screenshot four more times. On the fifth time, I think: the alternative is another Saturday of eating alone and bookmarking walks I'll never do. That thought is worse than any of the objections.*

**Booking**
*I sign up on a Sunday evening. I answer honestly. Non-drinker — not for any dramatic reason, I just stopped bothering a few years ago. No dietary requirements. Mid-range budget. Gender: male. "What do you want more of?" I tick Socialise and Food. I nearly untick Socialise because it feels too honest. I leave it. I enter my card details. I press confirm. And then I sit very still for about thirty seconds because I have just voluntarily signed up to have dinner with strangers, which is either the bravest or the stupidest thing I've done in years.*

**Waiting**
*Monday is normal. Tuesday afternoon I get a notification. My dinner is confirmed: a restaurant in the Northern Quarter, 7:30pm Wednesday. No names. No photos. Just a place and a time. My stomach does something. I look up the restaurant — it's got 4.4 on Google, small, looks warm. The menu's on their website. I read the entire menu. I'm not choosing in advance. I'm just giving myself something to do with the nervousness. I lay out clothes on Tuesday night. I change the jumper three times. I feel ridiculous. I also feel more alive than I have in weeks.*

**Showing up**
*Wednesday. I leave the flat at 7:05 even though it's a twelve-minute drive because if I sit in the flat any longer I'll talk myself out of it. I park. I walk to the restaurant. I'm early. I stand on the street pretending to look at my phone. A woman arrives at 7:25, looks at the door, looks at her phone, looks at me. "Come Join Us?" she says, and I nod, and she says "I'm Sarah" and I say "Brendan" and she says "I'm absolutely terrified" and I laugh — a real laugh — and say "same" and we walk in together. That small exchange. That admission of shared nervousness. It undoes something.*

**During dinner**
*Six of us. The table is round, which helps — no head of table, no hierarchy. There are questions on the table, printed on cards. The first one is "What's something you've been watching or reading that you'd recommend?" I answer. A nature documentary about octopuses. The guy across from me lights up — "the Netflix one? I watched that three times." And suddenly we're talking and it's not hard. It's not hard. Someone else mentions a book. Someone asks where I'm from. I say Chorlton, born and raised, and a woman named Amira says she's in Chorlton too. The food comes and it's good and the conversation doesn't stop and I realise, about an hour in, that I've said more words tonight than I normally say in a full weekend. Nobody is performing. Nobody is trying to impress anyone. We're just eating and talking and it's so ordinary and I could cry from how ordinary it is.*

**After dinner**
*I drive home at 10:15. The flat is quiet but it's different now. I put the kettle on. I sit at the two-person table and drink tea and think about the evening. I don't romanticise it — it wasn't a film. One of the guys was a bit dull, honestly. But Amira was interesting and the octopus guy was funny and Sarah asked me about my running and actually listened. I didn't perform. I was just there, being Brendan, and it was enough. I pick up my phone and open the Come Join Us app and opt in for next Wednesday. Then I open WhatsApp, find my mum's chat, and type: "Went for dinner with some people tonight. It was really nice." She'll be delighted. She'll ask a hundred questions. I don't mind. For once, I have answers.*

# QUICK MODE

---

I don't know when it happened exactly. There wasn't a moment where I went from having people to not having people. It was more like erosion. School friends drifted when they got girlfriends. Uni friends scattered. Work friends stayed at work. And then one day you look up and you're 34 and you live alone and your phone doesn't ring and you think, huh. So this is it then.

I'm not sad about it most of the time. That's the thing people wouldn't understand. It's not this dramatic, weeping-in-the-shower loneliness. It's quieter than that. It's eating dinner at a table with an empty chair opposite you. It's hearing a joke on a podcast and having no one to repeat it to. It's knowing the barista's name at the coffee shop near your flat and realising she might be the person you speak to most in a given week. It's mundane. That's what makes it heavy — it's so mundane that you can't even point at it and say "that, that's the thing that's wrong."

I cook, actually. Properly. I make roast dinners on Sundays. Full roast chicken, roast potatoes, the works. For one. I plate it nicely. I sit at the table. The food is good. I know it's good. But nobody else will ever know, and lately that's been bothering me in a way it didn't used to. I used to think I was independent. Self-sufficient. Now I think maybe I was just narrating a story about myself that made the loneliness liveable.

People say "put yourself out there" like it's turning a key. Like I haven't thought about it. I've thought about it at 3pm on a Saturday when the flat goes so quiet I can hear the fridge. I've thought about it while running past groups of people stretching together near the bridge. I've opened Google and typed "how to meet people" and stared at the results and closed the tab because every single option requires me to walk into a room full of strangers and be someone I'm not sure I know how to be anymore. Running clubs. Pub quizzes. Board game nights. They're all designed for people who already know how to do this. I don't know how to do this. I might never have known.

The worst part is the lying. Not big lies. Small ones. "Yeah, quiet weekend, caught up on some stuff." "Not much, just chilling." "I'm fine, Mum, yeah, I did go out actually." I lie because the truth is boring and uncomfortable and would make people worry in a way I don't want to manage. The truth is I went to the Trafford Centre to buy trainers I didn't need because I wanted to be around people without having to talk to any of them. The truth is I have a Reddit account with fourteen thousand karma because strangers on the internet are the only people I have real conversations with. The truth is I sometimes rehearse what I'd say at a dinner party, just in case, even though nobody has invited me to a dinner party in three years.

I think the thing I'm most afraid of is that I've left it too long. That there's a window for building a social life and I missed it, and now I'm just this guy. The quiet one. The one who's fine on his own. And everyone's accepted it, including me, and the acceptance is the trap.

But sometimes, late at night, or at 3pm on a Saturday, or when I see a photo of six people at a table laughing — sometimes the acceptance cracks, just for a second, and underneath it there's this thought that I can't shake: I don't want to eat alone forever.

That's all it is. I don't need a crowd. I don't need a best friend or a partner or a squad. I just need a table with some people at it, once a week, where I'm expected. Where someone would notice if I didn't show up.

That's such a small thing to want. I don't understand why it's so hard to find.
