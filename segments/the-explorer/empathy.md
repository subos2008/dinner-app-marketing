# DEEP MODE

## 1. Persona Snapshot

I'm Seb, I'm 31. I've been in Lisbon for five weeks — left my UX contract in London and went remote full-time back in January. I work from a coworking space in Santos three days a week and from cafes the other two. I've got a decent routine, a studio apartment in Alfama with a terrace, and a Portuguese SIM I'm weirdly proud of. I haven't had a conversation that lasted more than four minutes with anyone who isn't a client or a barista since I got here.

## 2. Empathy Map

### Think & Feel

- "I built this life on purpose. I'm not allowed to be lonely in it."
- Quietly terrified that the lifestyle he evangelises to friends is actually less fulfilling than the life he left
- Keeps a mental scoreboard: number of weeks in Lisbon vs. number of real connections made. The ratio is embarrassing.
- Notices he's started romanticising his London flatshare — the same one he couldn't wait to leave
- Feels a pang of something every time he walks past a group of friends eating outside and he's carrying a takeaway container back to his apartment
- Wonders if the other people at the coworking space have cracked something he hasn't, or if they're all pretending too
- Thinks about his ex-girlfriend's comment when he left: "You're running towards something or running from something." Refuses to examine which one it was.
- Finds himself mentally composing Instagram captions during experiences instead of actually having the experiences

### Say & Do

- **To friends back home on WhatsApp:** "Mate, the weather alone is worth it. 22 degrees in February. You're a mug for staying." (Sends photo of pastéis de nata and a sea view. Does not mention it's 7pm and he's eating them alone on a bench.)
- **To a coworking neighbour:** "Yeah just here for a few months, maybe more, we'll see." (Smiles. Returns to his screen. Puts his headphones back on. Interaction over.)
- **On Instagram:** Stories of rooftop sunsets, tram 28, the view from Miradouro da Graça. Never a selfie. Never a person. Nobody notices the absence.
- Downloads the Time Out Lisbon app, browses events, closes it. Downloads it again the next week.
- Eats lunch at the same cafe every day partly because the food's good and partly because the waiter recognises him and says "the usual?" — and that counts as a social interaction now
- Has walked into two bars alone, sat at the bar, nursed a beer, spoken to nobody, left. Told himself he was "soaking up the atmosphere."
- Calls his mum on Sundays. She asks who he's been spending time with. He talks about the city instead.
- Works longer hours than he did in London. Not because the workload increased — because there's nothing competing for the time.

### See

- Coworking space full of people who all seem to already know each other — inside jokes at the communal kitchen, weekend plans made at the coffee machine
- Instagram Reels of other nomads at group dinners in Bali, split-screen working-from-beach content that makes his solo cafe laptop setup feel inadequate
- Hostel bar crawl flyers pinned to noticeboards — not his scene anymore, but nobody's offering an alternative
- Couples and friend groups at every restaurant terrace in Bairro Alto on a Friday evening while he walks past with his headphones in
- His own apartment, which is beautiful and well-lit and completely silent by 8pm
- WhatsApp groups for "Digital Nomads in Lisbon" with 400 members and zero depth — "anyone know a good dentist?" level conversation
- The same four walls of his coworking desk, his apartment, his usual cafe, his usual supermarket — a city of half a million people reduced to a four-location loop

### Hear

- "You're living the dream, mate." (From people commuting to offices in the rain.)
- "Just go to a meetup! Nomads are everywhere in Lisbon, you'll make friends instantly." (He went to one. It was 30 people exchanging LinkedIn details and talking about their startups. He left after 40 minutes.)
- "Are you not lonely out there?" asked as a joke by friends, in a tone that makes it impossible to answer honestly
- The constant low hum of other people's conversations in the coworking space — close enough to hear laughter, far enough to not be part of it
- Podcasts. So many podcasts. They've replaced conversation. He listens to people talk for 8 hours a day and talks to almost nobody.
- His landlord, once a month, via text, in Portuguese he Google Translates
- The notification ping of Slack messages that are the closest thing to banter he has right now

## 3. Day in the Life — The Triggering Day

It's a Wednesday in early March. Seb wakes up at 8:10 to sunlight coming through the shutters he forgot to close. He reaches for his phone before his eyes are fully open. Three Instagram notifications — people reacting to his story of the sunset from São Jorge Castle yesterday. A thumbs up emoji from his old housemate Tom. A flame emoji from someone he went to uni with. He taps through. Nobody asked how he is. Nobody said anything that requires a reply. He puts the phone down and stares at the ceiling for a minute.

Coffee. He makes it with the Bialetti he bought his first week — one of those purchases that felt like a lifestyle statement. He drinks it on the terrace overlooking the rooftops. It's already warm. Laundry is drying on the rack beside him. He takes a photo of the coffee and the view, adds it to his story, types "office for the day" and then deletes it because he's posted the same thing four times this month. He puts his phone away and sits there for a moment in a silence that's either peaceful or empty, and he can't always tell the difference anymore.

He walks to the coworking space at 9:30. The route takes him through Alfama — narrow streets, azulejo tiles, a cat on a windowsill. He used to notice all of it. Now it's his commute. He passes a woman about his age carrying a yoga mat and thinks, briefly, that she looks like someone he'd be friends with. But there's no mechanism for that. You don't just stop someone on the street and say "do you want to get coffee sometime?" That's not how anything works.

The coworking space is busy. He nods at the guy who sits two desks over — a German developer whose name he thinks is either Max or Mats. They've had the same three-sentence exchange every morning for two weeks: "Morning." "Morning. Busy day?" "Always." Today Mats-or-Max is wearing a new t-shirt with a band Seb likes on it. Seb considers mentioning it. Doesn't. Puts his headphones on.

He works through the morning. A client call at 11 — 45 minutes of screen-sharing and discussing user flows. It's the most he'll speak today. After the call he goes to the kitchen to refill his water bottle and there's a group of four people laughing about something. Spanish, maybe. They're clearly here together — same company, or maybe just friends who travel together. One of them catches his eye and smiles. He smiles back, fills his bottle, returns to his desk. The interaction lasts six seconds and it's the warmest human moment of his day so far.

Lunch. He goes to his usual place — a small tascas on Rua de São Paulo. The waiter, Miguel, says "ah, the usual?" and Seb says "sim, obrigado" and feels a genuine flash of warmth because someone in this city knows his order. He eats grilled fish and rice at a table by the window. Alone. A couple next to him are sharing a bottle of vinho verde and arguing about where to go this afternoon. He catches himself eavesdropping and looks down at his phone.

He opens the WhatsApp nomad group. Someone's organising a "sunset drinks" thing on Friday. 47 people have reacted with a thumbs up. He knows what this is — it'll be a rooftop bar, everyone standing in clusters they arrived in, two hours of surface-level "where are you from? how long are you here? what do you do?" and then everyone leaves and nothing sticks. He went to the last one. He spoke to nine people. He remembers zero of their names. He doesn't react to the message.

Afternoon. Deep work, or something close to it. He designs three screens for a fintech client in Bristol who he's never met in person. At 4pm he takes a break, walks to the balcony of the coworking space. Lisbon is spread out below him — terracotta roofs, the Tagus glinting, a cruise ship in the distance. It's objectively stunning. He takes a photo, looks at it, and for the first time actively thinks: *who is this for?* He doesn't post it. He puts his phone in his pocket and goes back inside.

He finishes work at 6:15. Packs up his laptop. Mats-or-Max has already left. The Spanish group left at 5. The coworking space is emptying out and everyone seems to be going somewhere. He's going back to his apartment.

He walks home via the supermarket. Picks up chicken, some vegetables, a beer. The self-checkout doesn't even require him to speak. He walks up the hill to Alfama and passes three restaurants with outdoor seating, all filling up with the early dinner crowd. Groups. Always groups. He wonders where they all found each other.

Home. He cooks. The chicken is fine. He eats it at his small table with his laptop open, half-watching a YouTube video about someone else's van life in Portugal. The irony is not lost on him — he's watching a video about someone living his approximate lifestyle, alone, on a Wednesday night, while eating dinner by himself.

He cleans up. It's 8:30. The terrace is beautiful in the evening light. He sits out there with the beer and scrolls his phone. His friend Jake has sent a message to their group chat: a photo from a pub in Shoreditch, four of them crammed into a booth, captioned "missing you seb." He types "miss you boys" and adds a crying-laughing emoji and thinks about how that emoji has become a lid he puts on everything he actually feels.

He opens Instagram. Scrolls. A Reel about "the loneliness of the digital nomad lifestyle" has 2 million views. The comments are a support group: "I've been in Bali for three months and I have zero friends here," "the freedom is amazing until 7pm," "nobody talks about this part." He reads thirty comments. Every single one could be him. He screenshots the Reel. Opens WhatsApp. Looks at his conversations. Closes WhatsApp. Saves it to his camera roll instead.

He thinks about calling someone but it's 8:45 here, which is 7:45 in London, and everyone's either at dinner or out or doing the kind of Wednesday evening things that people with local friends do. He doesn't want to be the friend who calls because he has no one else to talk to. Even though that's exactly what he is.

At 10:30 he's in bed. The apartment is quiet except for fado music leaking through the wall from a neighbour. It's beautiful. It's the kind of thing he'd put on his story. Right now it just sounds like someone else's life happening nearby while his stays still.

He closes his eyes and the sentence arrives fully formed: *I didn't move to Lisbon to eat chicken alone on a Wednesday night.*

## 5. Emotional Journey

**Trigger**
*It's Wednesday night and I just ate chicken alone at my kitchen table watching a YouTube video of someone else living in Portugal. I've been here five weeks. I have spoken to Miguel the waiter more than anyone else this week. I'm not sad, exactly. I'm just — aware. Aware that I designed this life on purpose and I left out the most important part.*

**Search / Browse**
*I search "meet people Lisbon expat" and get the same results I always get. Language exchange meetups (I don't want to practise Portuguese, I want a friend). Pub crawls (I'm 31, not 21). The WhatsApp group sunset drinks (hard pass). A Facebook group where someone's posted "anyone want to grab a coffee?" three days ago with two likes and no replies. I close all the tabs and feel the specific frustration of being surrounded by options that don't fit.*

**Discovery (sees the ad)**
*I'm scrolling Instagram and something stops me. Not the usual travel content or coworking ad. It says something about dinner with 5 strangers, matched. The image is a restaurant table — nothing flashy, just a table with food and wine and people who look like they're actually talking to each other. The hook says something about not travelling this far to eat alone and it hits me in a way that's slightly uncomfortable, which probably means it's accurate.*

**Consideration**
*I tap through. They match you. Not randomly — they ask about dietary stuff, budget, drinking, age, and this one question: "what do you want more of in your life?" I sit with that for a second. What do I want more of? I tick Adventure and Socialise and, after a pause, Art. The matching feels real. Human-reviewed groups. Restaurant curated to the group. It's on a Wednesday, which is the night I just established is my worst night. I read the whole thing twice.*

**Objections**
*What if it's all other nomads and we just talk about client work and Notion setups for two hours? What if I'm the only one who's actually lonely and everyone else is just there for fun? What if the matching doesn't work and I end up with people I have nothing in common with? But then — what's my alternative? Another Wednesday night, same chicken, same YouTube video, same four walls? The worst case here is a mediocre dinner at a good restaurant. I've spent worse evenings on worse things.*

**Booking**
*I answer the questions. Vegetarian — no, I eat everything. Budget — mid-range, not backpacker but not blowing my monthly coworking fee on one meal. Alcohol — yeah, I drink, I like wine, I'm not precious about it. "What do you want more of?" Adventure, Socialise, Art. Gender — any mix is fine. I pay. Fifteen seconds of doubt, then a small hit of something that might be hope. I've actually done something. It's the first proactive social thing I've done since I got here that isn't reacting to a WhatsApp message with a thumbs up.*

**Waiting**
*Tuesday afternoon my phone buzzes. Restaurant name, address in Santos, 7:30pm tomorrow. A place I've walked past but never been to. No information about who's coming. Six people, and I'm one of them. I catch myself wondering who the other five are — where they're from, whether any of them are also eating dinner alone most nights. I try on two shirts in the mirror, which is absurd, but I want to make a good impression on people I haven't met for a dinner I signed up to through an app. I set an alarm for tomorrow even though I never need one.*

**Showing up**
*I'm walking to the restaurant and my palms are actually sweating, which is ridiculous because I once flew to a country where I didn't speak the language with no accommodation booked and I was fine. But this is different. This isn't an adventure — this is intimacy. I arrive two minutes early. There's a woman standing near the door checking her phone and looking around the way people do when they're trying not to look like they're waiting. I say "Dinner Matcher?" and she says "Oh thank god, yes" and we both laugh and walk in together and the ice is broken before we've sat down.*

**During dinner**
*Six of us. Three nationalities. One woman who's been in Lisbon for four months, a couple of locals who signed up because they wanted to meet new people (locals! I didn't expect that), a guy from São Paulo doing a masters, and a Scottish woman who arrived last week. The food is incredible — small plates, we share everything, someone orders wine for the table. Within twenty minutes someone asks "so what made you come to Lisbon?" and I tell the real version, not the Instagram version, and the woman across from me nods and says "same" and I feel something unlock in my chest. By the main course we're talking about things I haven't talked about in weeks — films, family, the weird guilt of leaving your country. Nobody has mentioned their job title. Nobody has asked for my LinkedIn.*

**After dinner**
*We stand outside the restaurant at 10:45 swapping Instagrams and someone says "same time next week?" and four people say yes immediately. Walking home I take a detour through Bairro Alto because I want the walk to last. The streets are full of people and for the first time in five weeks I feel like I'm one of them, not watching them. I get home and sit on the terrace and don't take a photo. I just sit there. I message Jake: "Had dinner with 5 strangers tonight through this thing, genuinely one of the best nights I've had here." He replies: "What?? You?? Tell me everything." I type for fifteen minutes. Real things. Not emoji lids.*

# QUICK MODE

---

It's the terrace that gets me. Everyone talks about the terrace. I talk about the terrace. I posted the terrace three times in the first week and people back home lost their minds — "living the dream mate," "you've cracked it," "so jealous." And yeah, the terrace is beautiful. The view is genuinely absurd. I can see the river and the bridge and the rooftops and there's bougainvillea growing up the wall and on a good evening the light goes this specific shade of gold that makes everything look like a film.

I sit out there every night. Alone. With a beer and my phone and the fado music coming through the wall from the neighbour I've never met.

It's week five and I've started counting. Not on purpose — it just happens. I count the number of real conversations I've had. Not client calls, not "the usual?" from Miguel at lunch, not Slack messages, not the three-sentence exchange with the German guy at coworking whose name I'm now too embarrassed to ask. Actual conversations. Where someone asks me something and listens to the answer and I ask them something back and we're just — talking. Like humans. The number this week is one, and it was with my mum on Sunday, and she did most of the talking because I don't know what to tell her. "Yeah it's great, the weather's incredible, work's busy." I've said that exact sentence four Sundays in a row.

The thing that nobody tells you about the nomad life — or maybe they do tell you, in those articles you scroll past because you think you'll be different — is that the loneliness isn't dramatic. It's not sitting in a corner crying. It's walking past a restaurant at 8pm and seeing a table of six people sharing wine and laughing and you're carrying a plastic bag from the supermarket back to your apartment to cook dinner for one. Again. It's the self-checkout. It's realising you've listened to nine hours of podcasts today because podcasts are your substitute for having someone to talk to. It's opening the WhatsApp nomad group and seeing "sunset drinks Friday?" and knowing exactly how it'll go — standing in a circle with people you'll never see again talking about where you're based and how fast your wifi is and pretending that's connection.

I went to one of those things two weeks ago. I spoke to a product designer from Berlin, a crypto guy from Miami, and a couple from Manchester who were doing three months around Europe. Nice people. Forgettable conversations. I left after an hour and got a kebab on the way home and sat on the terrace and thought: I have built an entire life around the idea of freedom, and I am using that freedom to eat a kebab alone and stare at a very nice view.

The thing I won't say out loud — the thing I barely think — is that I was less lonely in London. Sharing a mediocre flat in Hackney with Tom and Jas, fighting over the bathroom, eating bad pasta on the sofa watching whatever was on. I had people. The people were just there. I didn't have to earn them or find them or manufacture them. They were in the next room.

I keep almost telling someone. Almost typing the real thing in the group chat — "lads, I think I'm lonely" — and then I imagine the responses. "Come home then?" "You're in Lisbon mate, how can you be lonely?" "Go on Tinder lol." So I send a photo of the sunset instead and get fifteen fire emojis and that's the end of it.

Here's what I want. It's so simple it's almost embarrassing. I want to walk to a restaurant on a Wednesday evening and have someone be expecting me there. Not a date. Not a coworking happy hour. Not forty strangers at a rooftop bar. Just a table. A few people. Food we share. A conversation where nobody asks "so what's your stack?" I want to walk home at 10pm and feel like I belong in this city, even temporarily. Like I'm not just a tourist with a longer visa.

I'm 31. I have a beautiful apartment and a terrace and a view and total professional freedom and I am eating alone for the seventeenth Wednesday in a row. Something about that sentence should be funny. It isn't.
