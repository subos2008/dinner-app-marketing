# DEEP MODE

## 1. Persona Snapshot

I'm Marcus, I'm 33. I quit my account manager job eighteen months ago and started a consultancy helping hospitality businesses with their operations. It's going well — actually well, not "telling people it's going well" well. But the friends I've had since uni are still doing Friday pints at the same Wetherspoons, still talking about Fantasy Football and who's shagging who from the office. I love them. I genuinely do. I just can't keep pretending that's enough anymore.

## 2. Empathy Map

### Think & Feel

- "I don't want to be the guy who outgrew his mates. That sounds so arrogant. But something's off and I can't keep ignoring it."
- Guilty that he finds his oldest friends boring — he knows the problem might be him, not them
- Mentally composing business ideas in the shower, then realising he has nobody to tell who'd actually understand what he's talking about
- Quietly terrified that ambition is making him lonely and he'll end up successful and isolated
- Reads about founders and creatives who have these tight circles of people who push each other — wonders how you actually find that
- Thinks the phrase "your network is your net worth" is cringe but also maybe true and that makes him uncomfortable
- Catches himself performing two different versions of himself — downplaying the business around old friends, downplaying the old friends around new contacts
- Worries he's becoming judgemental. Doesn't want to be someone who ranks people by ambition.

### Say & Do

- **To old friends at the pub:** "Yeah the business is alright, ticking along." (It did 40k last quarter but saying that would make it weird.)
- **To his girlfriend:** "I just wish I had a couple of people who get it, you know? You get it, but I can't make you my entire world." She says she understands. She's relieved he's aware of it.
- **On LinkedIn:** Posts occasionally about the business. Gets likes from strangers. It's validation but it's empty — he wants a conversation, not a thumbs up.
- Listens to podcasts obsessively — Diary of a CEO, My First Million, The High Performance Podcast. Pauses episodes to think about what he'd say if he was in the room. Presses play again.
- Has been to two "networking events." Both were rooms full of people thrusting business cards at each other while pretending to care about what anyone else did. Drove home thinking "that's not it."
- Texts his mate Dan about a business article he read. Dan replies "sounds heavy mate haha." Marcus puts his phone down.
- Started going to a gym with a sauna because he heard someone say that's where good conversations happen. So far he's had good conversations about the temperature of the sauna.

### See

- Social media full of curated "mastermind" groups and "high-value networks" that cost three grand and look like pyramid schemes with better lighting
- His friends' Instagram stories: pub gardens, five-a-side, stag dos, the same faces he's known for a decade doing the same things they've done for a decade
- Podcasts guests casually mentioning "my mate who runs a fund" or "a friend of mine who sold his company" — people who apparently just *have* these connections
- Business Twitter/X full of people who seem to have found each other — co-working, collaborating, pushing each other — and he can't figure out where they met
- His own calendar: client calls, gym, pub with the lads, repeat. The shape of his weeks hasn't changed even though everything inside him has.
- Couples at restaurants on weeknights and he thinks: I want that, but the friend version. Just a regular thing with people I actually want to talk to.
- The "Suggested for You" on Instagram serving him entrepreneur content and self-improvement reels because the algorithm knows he's searching for something, even if he can't articulate what

### Hear

- "You've changed." (From friends. Said lightly, but it lands heavy.)
- "Don't lose your real mates chasing something that doesn't exist." (From his dad, who's had the same three friends since 1985 and can't fathom wanting more.)
- "You should come to this founder meetup." (From someone on LinkedIn he's never actually met.)
- "We never see you anymore." (He was there last Friday. They mean he's there but not *there*.)
- "Just be grateful for what you've got." (From everyone who doesn't understand that gratitude and restlessness can coexist.)
- The sound of his own voice explaining his business idea for the seventh time to friends who nod politely and change the subject to the football
- His girlfriend saying "you need your own thing, separate from me" — and knowing she's right and not knowing what that thing is
- Silence after he shares something he's excited about in the group chat

## 3. Day in the Life — The Triggering Day

Marcus wakes up at 6:40, ten minutes before his alarm. He's been doing this lately — waking up already switched on, head already running through client delivery for the day. He lies there for a minute, opens his phone, checks Stripe. A payment came in overnight from the restaurant group in Leeds. Fourteen hundred quid. He screenshots it. Hovers over the group chat, almost sends it, doesn't. They'd say "get you, Alan Sugar" and he'd have to laugh and downplay it and the moment would be gone.

He gets up, makes coffee, eats eggs at the kitchen counter standing up. His girlfriend Nic left for work at six — she's a physio, early starts. The flat is his until evening. He likes the mornings. The mornings are when he feels most like the version of himself he's becoming.

He works from the spare room they optimistically call "the office." Two client calls before lunch — one with a new restaurant in Ancoats, one with a hotel group he's been chasing for months. The hotel call goes well. Really well. They want a proposal by Friday. He hangs up and sits there for a second with his hands on his head and no one to tell. He texts Nic: "Hotel group said yes." She sends back a string of celebration emojis. He loves her for it. But she doesn't know what it *means* — the six months of follow-ups, the rewritten pitch deck, the two previous rejections.

He eats lunch at his desk. Scrolls Twitter. A thread about a guy who built a consultancy to seven figures catches his eye. He reads the whole thing. The replies are full of people tagging friends: "this is you" and "we talked about this." He has nobody to tag. He bookmarks it.

At 2pm he drives to a co-working space he uses twice a week. It's fine — good Wi-Fi, decent coffee. There's a designer called Sam who he nods at. They've had three conversations in four months, all about parking. Marcus has thought about asking Sam to grab lunch but it feels oddly high-stakes, like asking someone on a date. He doesn't ask.

He finishes work at 5:30 and goes to the gym. Legs day. He puts his AirPods in and listens to a podcast episode about building a personal board of advisors — five people you meet with regularly who challenge your thinking. The host makes it sound easy: "just find five people ahead of you who want to invest in your growth." Marcus literally laughs out loud on the leg press. Where? Where are these people? Is there a shop?

Home by 7. Nic's back. They cook together, eat together, it's nice. She tells him about a patient who cried during a session and how she didn't know what to do. He listens properly. She asks about his day and he gives her the headlines — hotel group, good calls, gym. He doesn't mention the podcast or the thread or the feeling he had at 11am with no one to tell. He's learned that putting all of that on her makes her feel like she's not enough, even though she is. She's his girlfriend. She shouldn't also have to be his mentor, his sounding board, his business peer, and his emotional outlet. That's too many jobs for one person.

At 8:30 the lads' group chat lights up. "Pub Friday?" The replies stack up: thumbs up, thumbs up, "deffo," "I'm there." Marcus types "yeah sounds good" and means about 60% of it. He knows exactly how Friday will go. He'll arrive at 8, they'll be two pints deep, someone will be telling a work story, they'll ask him how the business is and he'll say "yeah good" and they'll move on. He'll drive home at 11 feeling like he spent three hours with people he loves and still didn't have a single conversation that mattered.

He goes to the living room. Nic's watching something. He sits next to her but picks up his phone. Opens Instagram. An ad stops his scroll — not one of the usual "JOIN OUR MASTERMIND" things. Something about dinner with strangers. Matched. Wednesdays. He reads it twice. Almost taps through, then Nic asks him something and he puts his phone down.

But later, in bed, lights off, he's still thinking about it. Not the ad specifically. The feeling underneath it. The thing he can't say out loud to anyone because it sounds ungrateful: *I have friends. I have a girlfriend. I have a business that's actually working. And I'm still missing something. There are thoughts in my head that have nowhere to go.*

He falls asleep with his phone on his chest and the podcast app still open.

## 5. Emotional Journey

**Trigger**
*I'm sitting in the living room on a Wednesday night and Nic's watching telly and I've got this hotel proposal to write but I don't start it because I want to talk about it first. Not write it — talk about it. Say it out loud to someone who'll push back, ask questions, tell me what I'm missing. Nic would listen but she'd be being supportive, not being critical. I need critical. I need someone to say "that bit's weak, what about this instead?" And I realise: I don't have a single person I can call for that. Not one.*

**Search / Browse**
*I've looked before. "Entrepreneur networking Manchester." "Founders meetups." They're all either free events where you stand around with a name badge making painful small talk, or they're paid masterminds that cost two grand a quarter and sound like cults. There's a middle ground that apparently doesn't exist: just... meeting people. Normal, interesting people. Over food. Without an agenda. I've tried co-working spaces, I've tried events, I've tried being more social at the gym. Nothing sticks.*

**Discovery (sees the ad)**
*I'm scrolling Instagram and there's this ad. It's not shouting at me about networking or high performance or scaling my business. It just says something about dinner with 5 strangers, matched on things that actually matter. Matched. Not random. I nearly scroll past but then I see the line about Wednesday nights and I think: Wednesday. The nothing night. The night that's always just me and my laptop and a podcast. I tap.*

**Consideration**
*They ask real questions during sign-up. Budget — smart, because I don't need a fifty quid steak but I don't want to eat at a chain. Dietary — fine. Alcohol preference — I drink but it's not the point. And then this question: "What do you want more of in your life?" The options include business, adventure, art, sports, meditation. I tick business and adventure. Not because I want to network. Because I want to sit next to someone who chose the same things and find out why. There's a note about a human reviewing every group. That's the bit that gets me. Someone is actually thinking about who sits next to who.*

**Objections**
*What if it's a table full of people who just want to talk about themselves? What if it's actually a networking event disguised as dinner? What if I'm the only one who's there because he's outgrown his mates — and everyone else is just lonely? Then I think: what's the actual downside? I eat dinner at a restaurant on a Wednesday instead of eating dinner at home on a Wednesday. The floor is pretty high here.*

**Booking**
*I fill it in. Five minutes. Pay. It's done. I feel slightly ridiculous, like I've just admitted something. But also good. Like I've done something about the thing I've been circling for months instead of just listening to another podcast about it.*

**Waiting**
*Tuesday. I get a message: restaurant confirmed, 7:30, a place in the Northern Quarter I've walked past but never been to. No names. I tell Nic I'm going to "a dinner thing" on Wednesday. She says "oh nice, what dinner thing?" and I say "it's this thing where they match you with strangers" and she gives me this look that's half amused and half genuinely pleased, like she's been waiting for me to do something like this. "Good," she says. "You need that." It lands differently than I expected. Not embarrassing. Relieving.*

**Showing up**
*I get there at 7:25 and I'm stupidly nervous. I haven't had to introduce myself to strangers since freshers' week. I almost convince myself to leave but then a woman walks in and says to the host "I'm here for the group dinner?" and I think: OK, we're doing this. I follow her to the table. There are four chairs empty. Within ten minutes all six of us are seated. A management consultant, a graphic designer who just went freelance, a woman who runs a food truck, a software developer building a side project, and a trainee solicitor who looks as nervous as I feel.*

**During dinner**
*The table has conversation prompts but we barely use them because the food truck woman asks "so what made you sign up for this?" and suddenly everyone's being honest. The solicitor says she was bored of talking about law. The developer says he wanted to meet people outside of tech. I say "I started a business and realised I don't have anyone to talk to about it" and three of them nod like I just described their exact situation. By the main course I'm explaining the hotel proposal to the management consultant and she's poking holes in it — good holes, useful holes — and I feel this rush that I haven't felt since the early days of the business. Not excitement. Recognition. Being in a conversation where I don't have to translate.*

**After dinner**
*Walking to my car at 10:45. The air's cold and I feel wide awake. Not because the evening was life-changing — it was just dinner. But it proved something. There are people out there who think about the same things I think about. They're not in a podcast or on Twitter. They were just sitting at a table in the Northern Quarter because they also opted in on a Tuesday. I get in the car and sit there for a second before starting the engine. Then I text the lads' group chat: "Pub Friday I'm in." Because here's the thing — I don't want to replace them. I just needed to know I had somewhere else too.*

# QUICK MODE

...and the thing is I can't even explain it properly because it sounds so ungrateful. I have friends. Good ones. Lads I've known since we were eighteen, doing shots at the union and thinking we'd conquer the world. I love those guys. I'd do anything for them. But I sat in the pub last Friday and Tommo was telling the same story about his manager that he told three weeks ago and I caught myself just — drifting. Nodding in the right places but thinking about this pitch deck I need to rewrite and how I'd structure the pricing and whether I should lead with case studies or outcomes. And then I felt like shit because what kind of person zones out while their mate's talking? What kind of person mentally rewrites a proposal while someone's buying them a pint?

But that's the thing, isn't it? I haven't changed who I am. I've changed what I care about. And the two don't line up anymore. I started this business and suddenly my head is full of things I can't talk about. Not because they're secret — because nobody in my life is interested. I tried once. I told Dan about this client I landed and how I structured the deal and he went "mate, you sound like The Apprentice" and everyone laughed and I laughed too and then I just... stopped bringing it up. Now when they ask how the business is I say "yeah, good" and move on. Two words. That's what eighteen months of the hardest and most exciting thing I've ever done gets reduced to. Yeah, good.

Nic gets it more than anyone. She's proud of me, she listens, she asks questions. But I can feel myself leaning on her too hard. She's my girlfriend, not my advisory board. She shouldn't have to listen to me work through a pricing model at 10pm on a Tuesday. She has her own stuff. And I catch this flicker sometimes when I start talking about the business again — not annoyance, more like tiredness. She'd never say it. But I see it. And it makes me hold things back, which makes me feel more alone, which makes me lean on her more when I crack. It's a cycle and I know it's not healthy.

I listen to a stupid amount of podcasts. Like, an embarrassing amount. Diary of a CEO, My First Million, that one with the ex-Navy SEAL, whatever. And I've started to realise why. It's not because the content is that good. It's because those conversations — two people going deep on an idea, challenging each other, building on what the other person said — that's what I want. That's the thing I'm missing. Except I'm getting it through my AirPods instead of across a table. I'm eavesdropping on the kind of friendship I wish I had.

I went to a networking event last month. Drove twenty minutes, paid for parking, walked into a room full of people in lanyards holding warm white wine. Within five minutes someone had asked what I do, told me what they do, and tried to work out if we could be useful to each other. I lasted forty minutes. Drove home with the windows down feeling emptier than when I arrived. That's not what I want either. I don't want to be useful to someone. I want to be *interesting* to someone. I want a conversation where nobody's calculating the ROI of talking to me.

What I actually want is so simple it's almost embarrassing to say. I want to sit at a table with a few people who are building something — anything — and talk about it honestly. The wins and the bits that are terrifying and the 3am doubts and the things that went wrong. I want someone to say "have you thought about it this way?" and actually mean it. I want to leave a dinner feeling like I've got something to think about, not just something to digest.

I keep telling myself I'll find those people eventually. That it'll just happen. But I'm thirty-three and it hasn't happened yet. And I'm starting to think maybe it doesn't just happen. Maybe you have to go looking. I just don't know where to look that doesn't involve a lanyard or a two-grand mastermind or pretending I'm lonely when I'm not lonely. I'm something else. Something that doesn't have a good word for it. Restless, maybe. Hungry. Not for success — I've got that moving. Hungry for people who are hungry too.
