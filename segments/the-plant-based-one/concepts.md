## Concept 1: The One Sad Salad

**Insight:** From the day-in-the-life: the roasted cauliflower at the "contemporary British" restaurant. From quick mode: "I didn't go plant-based to eat roasted cauliflower at a steakhouse." The defining frustration isn't that she can't eat out — it's that she always eats the worst dish at the table.

**Emotional lever:** The specific, low-grade humiliation of ordering the one vegan thing on a menu designed for everyone else. Not anger — exhaustion. The performing that it's fine.

**Angle:** Name the dish. Everyone who's been the vegan at a non-vegan restaurant knows the cauliflower, the mushroom risotto, the side salad with the dressing removed. Call it out, then offer the opposite: a menu where everything is yours.

**Headline directions:**
- "You didn't go plant-based to eat the one roasted cauliflower at a steakhouse."
- "Mushroom risotto. Again. Page four. Under the heading 'Dietary.'"
- "The vegan option is chips. It's always chips."

**Format:** Feed ad (the frustration needs space to build). Also strong as a Reel with a reveal format.

**Test:** Specific dish callout (cauliflower, chips) vs general frustration ("the one thing you can eat"). Does naming the exact food make it funnier or too niche?

## Concept 2: The Bill-Splitting Maths

**Insight:** From quick mode: "doing maths in my head about how everyone else's meal cost £28 and mine cost £11 and we'll still split the bill equally because that's what groups do and bringing it up would make me the difficult one."

**Emotional lever:** The silent injustice that's too small to complain about but too constant to ignore. It's not about the money — it's about what the money represents: that the dinner wasn't built for her.

**Angle:** Lead with the maths. It's relatable, it's specific, and it's something she's never said out loud. Then pivot: at a table where everyone eats the same way, there's no subsidy, no imbalance, no performing.

**Headline directions:**
- "Their meal: £28. Yours: £11. The bill: split evenly. Every time."
- "You're subsidising other people's steak. And you can't even say it."
- "The bill-splitting maths nobody talks about."

**Format:** Feed ad. Also works as a Reel — the maths visual (receipt, split, eye-roll) is immediately readable.

**Test:** Direct financial angle ("£28 vs £11") vs emotional angle ("you can't even say it"). Does the money framing feel petty or feel seen?

## Concept 3: The Restaurant List

**Insight:** From the empathy map and day-in-the-life: "She has a list in her Notes app. Fourteen vegan restaurants in Manchester. She's been to two of them. Alone." From the emotional journey: the restaurant she gets matched to is one she's had saved on Instagram for months.

**Emotional lever:** The gap between having a life mapped out and having no one to live it with. The list is proof she loves food. The empty list is proof that loving food isn't enough without people.

**Angle:** Talk to the list. Everyone with dietary requirements in a city has one — the saved posts, the screenshots, the Notes app with restaurant names and zero plans attached. Then offer the missing piece: the people.

**Headline directions:**
- "You've got 14 vegan restaurants saved and no one to go with."
- "The list in your Notes app. The screenshots. The menus you've saved. All you're missing is the people."
- "We booked a restaurant from your list. (Well, it might as well be.)"

**Format:** Feed ad. Stories — screenshot-style visual of a Notes app list feels native to the platform.

**Test:** The specific number ("14 restaurants") vs the general behaviour ("restaurants you've saved"). Does the number make it more personal or more contrived?

## Concept 4: The Whole Menu Is Yours

**Insight:** From the emotional journey, waiting phase: "I look up the menu properly, not to check if I can eat there but to decide what I want. The whole menu. Everything on it." From empathy map: "Craves the feeling of sitting down and opening a menu where everything is for her."

**Emotional lever:** Joy. Not frustration, not exhaustion — the pure, simple delight of opening a menu and seeing that every dish was made for you. This is the aspirational version of the ad, not the pain version.

**Angle:** Lead with the feeling, not the problem. Skip the frustration and go straight to the promise. "A menu where everything is yours" is the emotional payoff every other concept builds toward.

**Headline directions:**
- "A dinner where the whole menu is yours."
- "Open a menu. Everything on it is for you. Not one section. Not one asterisk. Everything."
- "Every dish on the menu. Every person at the table. All on the same page."

**Format:** Feed ad. Stories/Reels — menu close-up, page turning, everything marked (V). The visual does the work.

**Test:** Aspirational ("the whole menu is yours") vs contrast ("not one asterisk, not one section — everything"). Does leading with joy outperform leading with frustration?

## Concept 5: Eating Dinner Twice

**Insight:** From the day-in-the-life closer: "Friday night. She ate dinner twice." She eats the cauliflower at the restaurant, then goes home and eats real food — the dhal she made on Wednesday. Two dinners: the performance and the reality.

**Emotional lever:** The absurdity of it. She went out to dinner and still had to eat at home because the dinner wasn't really for her. It's sad and funny in equal measure — and anyone who's been the vegan at a non-vegan table has done exactly this.

**Angle:** Name the double dinner. It's a behaviour she thinks is her own weird workaround, but it's universal among plant-based people. Calling it out creates instant recognition. Then: what if you only needed to eat once?

**Headline directions:**
- "Go to a restaurant. Eat the one thing you can. Go home. Eat again. Sound familiar?"
- "You shouldn't have to eat dinner twice."
- "The first dinner was for them. The second one, at home, was for you."

**Format:** Feed ad. Reel — before/after format (sad restaurant plate → warm home kitchen → then: one restaurant, one meal, done).

**Test:** Humorous angle ("sound familiar?") vs straight emotional ("you shouldn't have to"). Does the absurdity land as comedy or as a depressing callout?

## Concept 6: Veganism as Identity, Not Diet

**Insight:** From the profile: "being vegan is often a core identity, not just a food preference." From empathy map: "Proud of her values but exhausted by having to defend them in casual settings." From quick mode: "It's not a phase and it's not a diet and it's not a trend I'm going to grow out of."

**Emotional lever:** Respect. This ad doesn't treat veganism as a problem to solve or a box to tick. It treats it as who she is — and says: we built the matching around that.

**Angle:** Speak to the identity directly. Not "we accommodate vegans" but "what you eat is one of the first things we ask, and we actually use the answer." Make the matching the proof that her values aren't a footnote.

**Headline directions:**
- "Your diet isn't a limitation. It's how we match you."
- "We don't accommodate vegans. We match them."
- "What you eat is one of the first things we ask. And we actually use the answer."

**Format:** Feed ad. Also strong as a carousel: question 1 (what do you eat?) → question 2 (do you drink?) → question 3 (what do you want more of in your life?) → "then we match you with people who answered the same way."

**Test:** "We match them" (positioning) vs "We actually use the answer" (proof). Does the identity-first angle resonate more with longer-term vegans or newer converts?

## Concept 7: The Explaining Tax

**Insight:** From empathy map: "The silence when she asks the waiter what's vegan and the table waits, slightly impatient, for normal ordering to resume." From key phrases: "The best part wasn't the food. It was not having to explain the food." The constant, exhausting work of explaining, justifying, navigating.

**Emotional lever:** Relief. The ad promises the absence of a burden she's carried so long she's stopped noticing it. A table where nobody asks "can you eat bread?", nobody says "more for me then!", nobody requires an explanation.

**Angle:** Stack the micro-aggressions — not aggressively, just knowingly. "Can you eat bread?" "I could never go vegan." "More for me!" Then: a table where none of this happens. Not because you trained people. Because they already get it.

**Headline directions:**
- "'Can you eat bread?' — said by someone you've known for three years."
- "Nobody at this table will say 'I could never go vegan.' Because everyone at this table already did."
- "The best part isn't the food. It's not having to explain the food."

**Format:** Feed ad. Reel — stack the quotes as text on screen, then smash cut to a silent, happy dinner table where nobody's explaining anything.

**Test:** Stacked micro-aggressions (list format) vs single killer line ("not having to explain the food"). Does the stack build solidarity or feel like a rant?

## Concept 8: Not Vegan-Friendly. Vegan.

**Insight:** From quick mode: "There's a difference between a restaurant that has vegan options and a restaurant that's vegan. The first one is a concession. A checkbox." From key phrases: "The restaurant isn't vegan-friendly. It's vegan. The table isn't mixed. It's matched."

**Emotional lever:** Precision. She's spent years accepting "vegan-friendly" as the best she can get. This ad says: we don't do friendly. We do it. The restaurant is vegan. The table is vegan. Nothing is adapted, nothing is asterisked.

**Angle:** Draw the line between "vegan-friendly" and "vegan." One is a mushroom risotto on page four. The other is a chef who designed every dish on the menu for her. The product delivers the second one.

**Headline directions:**
- "Not vegan-friendly. Vegan."
- "The restaurant isn't 'vegan-friendly.' It's vegan. The table isn't mixed. It's matched."
- "A mushroom risotto on page four isn't a vegan option. It's a concession."

**Format:** Feed ad. Also strong as a single-line Story with heavy typography — "Not vegan-friendly. Vegan." is punchy enough to carry a frame alone.

**Test:** The two-word distinction ("Not friendly. Actual.") vs the fuller explanation (restaurant + table + matched). Does brevity outperform clarity?

## Concept Priority

1. **The One Sad Salad** — The most immediately relatable concept. Every plant-based person has been the cauliflower person. It names the core frustration in a way that's specific, slightly funny, and instantly recognisable. First ad to write and test.

2. **The Whole Menu Is Yours** — The aspirational counterweight to Concept 1. Where the salad is the pain, the whole menu is the promise. Together they form the before/after of the campaign.

3. **Not Vegan-Friendly. Vegan.** — The strongest product-truth ad. Draws a clear line between what exists everywhere and what Dinner Matcher specifically delivers. Good for people who already know the problem and need to trust the solution.

4. **The Explaining Tax** — Universal recognition for anyone who's been the vegan at a mixed table. The micro-aggressions stack creates solidarity fast. Strong second-wave ad.

5. **The Restaurant List** — Emotionally potent because it speaks to something private and specific. The Notes app list is a behaviour the target does daily. Works well on Instagram where the visual is native.

6. **Eating Dinner Twice** — The most narrative concept. Needs slightly more space to land but the absurdity is memorable. Good for Reels.

7. **The Bill-Splitting Maths** — A strong supporting angle. Says something she's never said out loud. Works best paired with a broader concept rather than as a lead.

8. **Veganism as Identity** — Important positioning but more abstract than the food-specific concepts. Best used as body copy or as the differentiator within other ads rather than leading a campaign on its own.
