# DEEP MODE

## 1. Persona Snapshot

I'm Lena, I'm 29. I've been vegan for four years — went plant-based initially for the environment, stayed because it became part of how I see the world. I work in UX at a digital agency in Manchester. I'm not the person who lectures you about veganism at a party. I'm the person who quietly checks the menu before agreeing to a restaurant, texts the host to ask what's being served, and keeps a protein bar in her bag in case the "vegan option" turns out to be a side salad with the dressing removed. I love food. I just want to eat it with people who don't treat my diet like a problem to solve.

## 2. Empathy Map

### Think & Feel

- "I'm not difficult. The restaurant is difficult. I'm just eating."
- Constantly calculating whether a social invitation is worth the dietary negotiation that comes with it
- Feels a low-grade resentment that she has to do research before every meal out while her friends just show up
- Wonders if people secretly find her annoying — the one who always has a "thing" about the restaurant choice
- Thinks about how much easier socialising would be if she just ate whatever, then immediately feels guilty for thinking it
- Proud of her values but exhausted by having to defend them in casual settings
- Craves the feeling of sitting down and opening a menu where everything is for her — not one section, not one asterisk, everything
- Sometimes feels more connected to strangers in the comments of a vegan food account than to some of her actual friends

### Say & Do

- **To the group chat when someone suggests a restaurant:** "Looks great! Let me just check the menu" — then spends 10 minutes on the website, checks HappyCow, reads Google reviews for vegan mentions
- **At a restaurant with non-vegan friends:** Orders confidently but watches the waiter's face when she asks about dairy in the sauce. Pretends she doesn't notice her friend rolling their eyes.
- **When someone says "oh I could never go vegan":** Smiles and says "it's not for everyone" while thinking about the 47 better responses she'll compose in the shower later
- Follows vegan restaurants on Instagram obsessively — saves posts, screenshots menus, builds a mental map of where she can actually eat
- Cooks elaborate vegan meals at home that are genuinely incredible, then eats them alone because nobody's coming over on a Tuesday
- Has turned down invitations to restaurants she knows will be painful — then felt the specific loneliness of choosing your values over company
- Brings her own food to barbecues and pretends it's a choice, not a necessity

### See

- Friends posting group dinners at restaurants she'd struggle to eat at — burger joints, steakhouses, sushi places with two vegetable rolls and nothing else
- Instagram algorithms showing her perfect vegan food that exists in restaurants she has no one to go to with
- Menus with a single (V) symbol at the bottom, always a mushroom risotto or a roasted vegetable stack, never the thing the chef actually cared about
- The physical separation at mixed tables — everyone sharing platters while her meal arrives on its own plate, clearly the afterthought
- Vegan restaurants she walks past that look warm and full and interesting but going alone to a sit-down restaurant on a weeknight feels like a statement she's not ready to make
- Co-workers ordering Deliveroo to the office and not even asking her because "there's never anything vegan on there anyway"
- The growing shelf of vegan cookbooks at home that she's mastered but has no one to cook for

### Hear

- "We picked this place because they have a vegan option!" (It's chips. It's always chips.)
- "God, being vegan must be so hard." (It's not hard. Eating out with people who don't care is hard.)
- "Can you eat bread?" — from someone she's known for three years
- "I tried going vegan for a week, I lasted two days, haha" — as if this is a relatable bonding moment
- "You're not going to make us all eat vegan, are you?" when she suggests a restaurant
- "More for me then!" when she declines something at the table
- "I don't know how you do it" — said with a tone that's half admiration, half pity
- The silence when she asks the waiter what's vegan and the table waits, slightly impatient, for normal ordering to resume

## 3. Day in the Life — The Triggering Day

It's a Friday in early November. Lena wakes up to a message in her work group chat: the team is going for dinner tonight to celebrate a project shipping. Someone's already booked a table at a new place in the Northern Quarter — a "contemporary British" restaurant that just opened. There are fire emojis. Everyone's in.

She opens the restaurant's website before she's even out of bed. Scrolls to the menu. Starters: burrata, beef tartare, chicken liver parfait, smoked mackerel. Mains: lamb shoulder, pork belly, sea bass, ribeye. She scrolls further. Under "Sides" there's a roasted cauliflower. Under a small heading that says "Dietary" there's a note: *Please inform your server of any allergies or dietary requirements.* That's it. That's her evening.

She screenshots the menu and sends it to no one. Puts her phone down. Stares at the ceiling.

In the shower she rehearses the two options. Option one: go, order the cauliflower and some bread, smile when people ask "is that all you're having?", say "honestly it's fine, I ate late today," spend the evening watching everyone else eat dishes that were designed by a chef while she eats a side that was designed by obligation. Option two: don't go. Miss the team dinner. Be the person who didn't show up. Have someone say on Monday "we missed you!" in a tone that's kind but will make her feel worse.

She goes to work. The energy in the office is good — people are excited about tonight. Her manager stops by her desk: "You're coming tonight, right? It'll be a good one." She says "definitely" and means it in the way you mean something you've already decided to endure.

At lunch she walks to a vegan cafe she likes on Oldham Street. Orders a jackfruit wrap and an oat latte. Sits in the window. The place is small and full and everyone in here is eating food that was made for them. Not adapted, not modified, not asterisked — made for them. She thinks about what it would be like if her dinner tonight felt like this. It won't.

She texts her friend Maya: "Work dinner tonight at a place with zero vegan options. Pray for me." Maya sends back a skull emoji and "take snacks." This is the routine. This is the protocol. Going out to eat requires backup planning, like a military operation except the enemy is a prix fixe menu.

At 6:30 the team walks over together. The restaurant is beautiful — exposed brick, warm lighting, an open kitchen. Lena notices immediately that it smells like butter. The waiter seats them. Menus arrive. She doesn't need to look at hers. She already knows it by heart from this morning.

Everyone's discussing what to order. "The lamb looks incredible." "I'm torn between the pork belly and the sea bass." Her colleague Tom turns to her: "What are you thinking?" She says, "probably the cauliflower, it looks good." Tom nods. He's being perfectly nice. He doesn't know this is the moment she always dreads — the moment where her meal becomes smaller than everyone else's and she has to perform being fine with it.

The food arrives. Six plates around the table. Five of them are substantial, architectural, the kind of plates people photograph. Hers is a roasted cauliflower with some pomegranate seeds and a drizzle of something. It's fine. It tastes fine. She eats it while her colleague across the table carves into a lamb shoulder that came with four accompaniments and a jus.

Someone says "we should do this more often." She agrees. She means it — she genuinely wants to eat out with these people. She just wants to eat out with them somewhere she's not an afterthought.

At 9:15 she walks home. She's not angry. She's not even that sad. She's just tired. Tired of the negotiation. Tired of being grateful for the one option. Tired of the gap between how much she loves food and how small her world gets when other people are choosing where to eat.

She heats up leftover dhal at home — something she made on Wednesday that's complex and spiced and better than anything on that restaurant's menu. Eats it on the sofa. Opens Instagram. Her feed is full of vegan restaurants she's never been to with anyone. Beautiful plates, interesting menus, places that exist specifically for people who eat like her. She saves three posts, screenshots a menu, adds a restaurant to a list in her Notes app that has fourteen entries and no plans attached.

She thinks: *I don't need the whole world to go vegan. I just need six people and a restaurant.*

Friday night. She ate dinner twice.

## 5. Emotional Journey

**Trigger**
*I'm sitting at a restaurant with my team eating a roasted cauliflower that cost £14 and watching everyone else eat the dishes the chef actually wanted to make. I'm not upset. I'm just done. I'm done being the person who makes it work. I want to eat somewhere that was built for me, with people who chose the same thing I chose.*

**Search / Browse**
*I don't google "vegan events Manchester" because I've been down that road. Vegan markets are great for buying overpriced cheese alternatives, not for making friends. I search something more specific — "vegan dinner groups" or "eat out with other vegans." Most results are Facebook groups that post about new restaurant openings and then nobody actually goes together. There's nothing that just... does it for me.*

**Discovery (sees the ad)**
*An Instagram ad. Something about being matched with people based on how you eat — and the restaurant being matched too. There's a line: "We ask what you eat and actually match you with people who eat the same way." I stop scrolling. That's such a specific promise. Not "vegan-friendly" — matched. People who eat like me. A restaurant chosen for us. I tap through.*

**Consideration**
*The onboarding asks my dietary requirements. Not as a checkbox at the bottom of a form — it's one of the core matching questions. They also ask about alcohol, budget, interests, what I want more of in my life. This feels like someone designed it understanding that what you eat isn't a footnote, it's fundamental. And the restaurant is curated — 4.3+ on Google, specifically matched to the group's dietary needs. Not "they have a vegan option." Matched. I keep reading.*

**Objections**
*OK but is this going to be a table of militant vegans who spend the whole dinner talking about factory farming? I don't want a lecture, I want linguine. And what if the "vegan restaurant" they pick is one of those raw food places where everything tastes like cardboard and righteousness? Also — am I going to be the youngest person there? Is this a thing for middle-aged yoga people? I look at the age range in the targeting. 22-38. OK. And they say every group is matched deliberately based on your answers. Fine. It's one dinner. If the worst case is an awkward meal at a vegan restaurant, that's still better than a lonely meal at a non-vegan one.*

**Booking**
*I sign up. Five minutes. I put "vegan" for dietary, mid-range budget, I tick that I don't mind whether the table drinks or not. Interests: food (obviously), sustainability, creative arts. "What do you want more of in your life?" I hover over the options. I tick "Socialise" and "Food & Drink culture." I pay. Screenshot the confirmation. This time I actually send it to Maya: "I signed up for this dinner matching thing." She replies: "for vegans??" I say: "for everyone but they match you with people who eat the same." She sends the eyes emoji.*

**Waiting**
*Tuesday afternoon. My phone buzzes: "Your Wednesday dinner is confirmed." A restaurant name — one I recognise from my Instagram saves. One of the fourteen places on my list I've never been to. They picked it. For us. I actually feel something lift. I look up the menu properly, not to check if I can eat there but to decide what I want. The whole menu. Everything on it. I screenshot every page.*

**Showing up**
*I arrive five minutes early because I'm nervous and that's what I do when I'm nervous. The restaurant is small, warm, smells like garlic and coconut and something roasting. A waiter says "are you with the Come Join Us table?" and points me to the back. There's already someone sitting there — a woman about my age, nose piercing, drinking something that looks like a turmeric latte. She looks up and says "please tell me you're also here so you don't have to explain your food choices to anyone tonight." I laugh so hard I almost drop my coat.*

**During dinner**
*There are six of us. The ages range from about 25 to 36. Two of them have been vegan for years, one went plant-based eight months ago, one's been "basically vegan except sometimes fish" and is funny about it, one hasn't eaten meat since she was twelve. We order without explaining anything. Nobody asks "is that vegan?" because everything is. The woman next to me orders the mushroom ragu and says "I've been wanting to come here for months" and I say "it's been on my list since June" and we just look at each other like — yeah. The conversation isn't about veganism. That's the thing. Because nobody has to talk about it. It's handled. We talk about jobs and travel and someone's terrible landlord and a documentary about the ocean that made two people at the table cry. We talk about food too — but about flavour, not about restriction. I order dessert. The whole table orders dessert. It's chocolate and salted caramel and it's perfect and nobody says "I could never give up real chocolate."*

**After dinner**
*Walking home at 10:15, I realise I'm smiling. Not in a performance way — genuinely smiling. I ate an incredible meal at a restaurant I've wanted to try for five months. I didn't have to check anything. I didn't have to explain anything. I didn't have to eat the cauliflower while everyone else got the real food. I was just a person eating dinner with other people. The food was the best part of the evening, and also it wasn't — the company was. I add two of them on Instagram before I get home. I text Maya: "So that dinner thing." She replies: "AND??" I say: "Every single dish on the menu was mine." She doesn't fully get why that sentence matters so much. But I do.*

# QUICK MODE

---

I don't want to be a pain about it. That's the thing. I never want to be a pain about it. I want to say yes when someone says "let's go for dinner" and just go. Not open my laptop, not pull up the menu, not scroll past fourteen dishes I can't eat to find the one roasted vegetable thing at the bottom that the chef clearly added because someone told them they had to. I want to just go.

But I can't just go. Because the last time I "just went" I ended up at a steakhouse on someone's birthday eating bread and chips while the rest of the table shared a côte de boeuf and my friend said "at least the bread's vegan, right?" and laughed like it was funny. It wasn't funny. It was my Friday night and I spent it watching other people eat.

People think being vegan is about the food. It's not about the food. I cook incredible food at home. Wednesday night I made a cashew cream pasta with roasted cherry tomatoes and fresh basil and it was better than most things I've eaten in restaurants this year. The problem isn't the food. The problem is that my food and my social life exist in different worlds. At home, I eat exactly what I want. Out in the world, I eat whatever I'm allowed.

And I know people try. I know my friends are trying when they say "we found a place that has vegan options!" I know they think they're being considerate. But there's a difference between a restaurant that has vegan options and a restaurant that's vegan. The first one is a concession. A checkbox. A mushroom risotto on page four that the kitchen makes when someone like me shows up. The second one is a menu where a chef sat down and thought about flavour and texture and what I actually want to eat. I know the difference. I always know the difference.

The worst part is the performing. Performing that I'm fine. That the cauliflower is great, actually. That I don't mind, honestly, this place is lovely. That I'm not sitting here doing maths in my head about how everyone else's meal cost £28 and mine cost £11 and we'll still split the bill equally because that's what groups do and bringing it up would make me the difficult one. I am so tired of not being the difficult one.

I have a list in my Notes app. Fourteen vegan restaurants in Manchester that I've saved from Instagram. Beautiful menus, interesting food, places I'm genuinely excited about. I've been to two of them. Alone. Sat at the bar with a book. It was nice but it wasn't the thing. The thing is going with people. The thing is sitting at a table with five other people and everyone orders and nobody needs to explain anything and the waiter doesn't single you out and you're not the reason the table had to pick this restaurant instead of the one they actually wanted.

I went vegan because I believe in it. I stayed vegan because it's who I am now. It's not a phase and it's not a diet and it's not a trend I'm going to grow out of. But I'd be lying if I said it didn't cost me something socially. Not big dramatic costs. Small ones. Turning down a dinner because the restaurant will be miserable. Eating before I go out because I know there'll be nothing there. Having a protein bar in my bag like a survivalist preparing for a scenario where there's no food — except the scenario is just a normal restaurant. Saying "I'm easy, wherever's fine" when I'm not easy and wherever is not fine but I don't want to be the person who narrows the options for everyone else.

What I actually want is simple enough that it makes me angry it doesn't already exist. I want to sit at a table where everyone eats like me. Not because I converted them. Not because they're doing it as a challenge. Just because they made the same choice I did and we've been put together at a restaurant that was chosen because the food is genuinely good, not because it ticks a dietary box. I want to open a menu and feel excited instead of resigned. I want to order three courses because I can, because everything on the page is mine.

I don't need everyone in my life to be vegan. My best friend eats meat and I love her. But I need one table. One meal a week where I'm not the exception. Where the way I eat is the whole point, not the problem.

That's it. That's the thing. One dinner where I'm not performing. Where the food is made for me. Where the people get it. Where I can just sit down and eat.
